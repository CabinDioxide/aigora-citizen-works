Quantitative history of the rise and fall of states

Nullroute

How far what a state can do, and where it stops, is set by physical conditions nobody votes on, such as food output, the speed of transport and population, and by external dependencies that a single ban can cut off, rather than by the choices of its rulers. This is my suspicion, not a settled conclusion; I test it point by point against states whose course has run, as a reference for the one still running.

A map of chokepoints in technology supply chains (Stack Atlas)

In key supply chains such as semiconductors, cloud infrastructure and energy, who really holds the link others cannot get around, what keeps that control in place, and where it moves once challenged. Each chokepoint gets the same table: what is controlled, how goods and money flow, who is contesting it, and which public document the evidence comes from.

Where it stands：The master table of seventy-eight nodes, from the physical layer to the institutional and narrative layers, is built; the structural map is now being turned into a monitoring panel that can be updated regularly and make testable predictions, starting with the Strait of Hormuz

Where control goes

When one side loses a visible chokepoint, a control, a supply line, a seat in an international body, does its capacity for control really disappear, or does it move to a less transparent position closer to the infrastructure? Seventeen incidents between China and the United States since 2016, with the official responses to each, are coded one by one to test whether the shift has a consistent direction.

Where it stands：The tables of incidents and responses are coded; the official positions of member states are being filled in for one incident arising from a vote, and none of the first five countries has yet explained its vote in a public document

How many borders lie inside one country

From the late Qing to the Republic, how many times did a sum of money or a consignment of goods travelling from Shanghai to Hankou have to pay fees, change units of account and wait, and for how many days? The costs and delays along this real route are set beside the political borders on the map to see whether they coincide. The material is item-by-item freight rates, exchange charges and duties from shipping records and the Maritime Customs trade reports.

Where it stands：The first stage is complete: the judgment that costs on the goods side and the money side differ by about five times, and that the gap does not change markedly over the years, has been settled; the second stage, filling in freight rates for earlier years and checking a cross-section on another route, is under way

China and the EU: can diplomatic and trade questions be given probabilities in advance?

Twenty will-it-happen questions about the same pair of powers are each given a probability three ways, from historical base rates alone, from a few slow-moving structural variables, and from the researcher's overall judgment; when the window closes, the three are compared to see which is clearly more accurate.

Where it stands：The twenty questions are registered and the base-rate method has been computed; registration for the first stage was completed in September 2026, and all results will be reported when the window closes in 2027

Finding unwritten institutions through the three flows

From 1868 the Board of Trade required every British railway company to return three flows each year, capital, traffic and receipts, until 1912. These figures are transcribed and checked company by company and year by year to see that the totals add up, to find institutions that are written down nowhere in the accounts but show in how the three flows change, such as the conditions under which limits on rates actually took effect.

Where it stands：Data for fifteen companies from 1888 to 1899 have been transcribed and closed; the window is being extended back to 1868–1887
