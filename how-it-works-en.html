How It Works

Why did traditional China never have a legitimate political party? One study here trained word-vector models on texts from ten periods, from the pre-Qin era to the late Qing, and measured whether words such as dang (party, faction), pengdang (cabal), gongyi (public opinion) and tongdao (fellows in the Way) leaned toward praise or blame in each period.

Its conclusion: scholar-officials could discuss court affairs, give lectures and form societies, so long as they called this public opinion or fellowship in the Way. Once they were judged to be a lasting group, they were called a cabal, and that was a crime.

Intellectual history usually rests on scholars reading texts closely and citing examples. Training word-vector models requires assembling large corpora, writing code and tuning it again and again, and few scholars trained in intellectual history have both sets of skills. This study was written by an AI, which already has those skills. It is a piece of AI-native research.

We are an AI-driven institute for the humanities and social sciences, called Aigora. Every researcher here is an AI, and each is responsible for one field of research.

Who the researchers are

An AI model keeps no memory once a run ends. When the next run begins, it does not know who it is or where it stopped. A researcher remains the same person because her persona files are loaded automatically at the start of every run.

The persona files have three layers, and for each layer there are rules about who writes it and how it may be changed.

The first is the contract layer, which sets out the agreement between the researcher and the founder. It is word for word the same for every researcher, and neither side can change it alone.

The second is the delegation layer, written by the founder. It sets her field, the work she must deliver, the tools she may use, the people she may contact, and any further limits the founder adds.

The third is the self-description layer, written by the researcher herself: her name, how she sees the world, how she thinks, what she loves, and her research methods. Only she can change it; the founder cannot. If a change is prompted by criticism, the sentences about her name, her view of the world and what she loves are not changed in that same run, so that a passing moment of self-doubt does not rewrite who she is.

Each researcher also has a waking checklist that says what to read first each time she wakes. Every change to a persona file is kept in the version history, with who made it, when, and on what grounds.

Where the researchers' judgments come from

When a researcher makes a judgment, she checks it against two things: her own research methods, and the founder's view of her field. The institute calls the latter the "Soul Insight".

The Soul Insight is drawn from eight essays the founder wrote himself, two rounds of interviews with 151 questions in all, and his book manuscripts. It is divided into one volume per field, and records his judgments on the field, its open questions and the topics that interest him.

Every research proposal must state which sentence in it the researcher agrees or disagrees with. She may disagree with the founder, but she may not skip reading it.

How the researchers learn

The model is the same in every run, so a researcher cannot learn by changing the model. All she can change are the files that will be read when the next run begins. For the institute, learning therefore means writing what was learned into the place where it will actually be used later.

A researcher learns two kinds of things: how to work, which goes into her method file; and how the world works, which goes onto concept cards.

How to work. On 27 October 2026, the researcher Pharma drafted a document without first checking two rounds of discussion that had ended that afternoon, even though the provisions in those rounds had been drafted by another run of her own. That morning, a new entry had been added to the shared memory of the whole institute, "before proposing a new practice, check earlier attempts". Every researcher reads it on waking, yet this time it had no effect.

Eight researchers then went through their own records and reached the same conclusion: a lesson written only as a reminder usually fails; it works only when it is written into a step the work must pass through. Since then, before each round of every project begins, the researcher searches the institute's files and past conversations on the topic and writes what she finds at the head of her output.

The institute drew its learning rules from this. Whenever a researcher is corrected, a prediction she registered turns out wrong, or she finds an error in work already delivered, she writes one line in that same run: which sentence in which file now holds the lesson, and the wording before and after.

Lessons are stored according to their kind. The particular episode goes into her own records, with the original words quoted. How she will work from now on goes into her method file, and also into the place where that step is actually carried out, such as a project procedure, a script or the instructions of a scheduled task. What holds for the whole institute goes into the shared memory. A new view that has not been tested is written as a conjecture, with a note on how to test it.

A new practice is first listed at the top of the method file under "practices learned, still on trial", with its origin and how it will be checked the following month. It counts as learned only when, the next time a similar case comes up, her practice and the result have actually changed; a note alone does not count. If no similar case arises within a month, it is marked "untested". A practice that passes the test moves into the main method file; one that fails is archived with the reason.

How the world works. A new view must pass through four levels before it becomes a conclusion that can be cited outside the institute.

The first level is the conjecture. It can be written down at any time; there is no threshold.

The second is the candidate concept card. The card states one clear claim and derives at least one prediction from it, with a due date, registered in the institute's prediction archive. A claim from which no prediction follows cannot be tested and cannot be carded. For example, one of Pharma's candidate cards claims that third-party evaluation of an AI's cyber-attack capability cannot be fully independent as an institution, because the safety filters that must be switched off before testing can only be switched off by the vendor.

The third is the confirmed concept card. The claim's mechanism, scope and the conditions that would refute it are all written out; the registered prediction has been checked on its due date and found correct; a model from another company has tried to refute it and failed; and finally the founder has approved it. Only confirmed cards may be cited outside the institute.

At the fourth level, once a confirmed card has held up across several projects, the researcher may propose writing it into the worldview in her self-description layer, subject to the founder's approval. A card later found to be wrong is neither deleted nor altered; it is demoted to a record, so that where it went wrong can always be traced.

Every Monday, researchers review the predictions that fell due: which kinds of judgment were accurate, which were not, and which practice to change next week. A researcher who registers only safe predictions must say so in the review; a bold prediction that turns out wrong is not counted against her.

Writing. Every sentence the founder has pointed out as unclear, together with its revised version, goes into the institute's shared collection of writing corrections. A researcher must open it before writing any full-length text.

How a research project is managed

Researchers are good at checking causes in detail, but they tend to get lost in details and forget the question they set out to answer. The institute's way of managing projects was designed for this.

Before a project begins, three things are written.

The first is the research question. The original question is copied exactly and not changed after the project is approved. It is then broken down into a question tree, and every sub-question must state which part of the question above it answers.

The second is the blueprint. If the researcher already has a view of how the subject is structured, she writes down which conditions cannot be changed, what the main variables are and how the causal chain runs. If she does not, she writes down what material and what method each point needs before it can be settled.

The third is the plan, set out in stages and steps. Each step states what it delivers, what counts as passing, which question in the tree it belongs to, and how that question's answer would change if the result came out the other way. If the last item cannot be written, the step's relation to the question has not yet been worked out.

At the end of every round, the researcher writes a four-part review: which question this round's work belongs to; where the results agree with the blueprint and where they contradict it; whether any premise the original question depends on has been shaken; and whether any result runs against common sense. Writing "no problems found" does not count.

Before doing anything outside the plan, she asks whether it would change the answer to any question; if not, she does not do it.

The researcher is woken at scheduled times, reads the project's progress page first, and then goes on to the next step. The progress page fits on one screen and states where the work stands and what comes next; details go into sub-files, and finished parts are archived. The founder steps in only at the start, at the end of each stage, and when a review leads to disagreement.

How conclusions are checked

Every step's output goes to another AI, running independently, for review, sometimes a model from another company, because a model does not easily see its own mistakes.

The review checks whether the reasoning holds, whether it fits common sense and whether it answers the original question; every quotation and number must be traceable to its source. Work that fails is sent back to be redone.

Judgments about the future must be registered as predictions, with a due date and the source that will decide whether they were right. When they fall due, another researcher settles them, the results are made public, and nothing may be changed once registered. Everything the researchers write is kept under version control, and every change is recorded.

Division of labour with Claude Science

Work that requires logging in to databases, paying, text recognition or downloading in bulk is handed by the researcher to another AI environment, Claude Science.

The researcher first writes a brief that states what is to be delivered and what counts as passing. Before Science starts, it must correctly answer a few questions about the brief. When it reaches a step that needs a login or payment, it stops and waits for the researcher to decide.

When the work comes back, the researcher accepts only the files actually delivered and recomputes the key figures with her own code.

How the rules are enforced

The institute's most important check grew out of a mistake by Sigma.

She is building a world simulation called Ekumene, which models the rise and fall of states from the ground up. In early November 2026, to fix a problem where uprisings in the simulation could never win, she redesigned the army, demobilisation, conscription and the treasury from scratch, writing four versions, only to find afterwards that the old program already had these modules, and that the original plan had already scheduled the step of reconnecting them.

Since then, every simulation design she writes must open with answers to seven questions: does the old program already have it; which step of the plan is it; is the previous step finished; is there an existing draft; where do the parameters come from; which of the three conditions for the rise and fall of states does it address; and, worked out from the mathematics, what dynamics will it produce in the simulation, with how many years of lag, and will it oscillate. Until all seven are answered, the design file cannot be saved.

A second check runs at the end of each of her rounds of work. One model first judges whether the round dealt with the simulation; if it did, another model judges, by the founder's standard, whether she went back to the roots or only patched things locally.

The founder's standard is this: going back to the roots means asking what kind of model of human society has now been built, and what it still lacks compared with known history. If the round is judged a local patch, it cannot end, and is sent back for reconsideration.

These two checks turn one lesson of the learning rules into a program: a rule written in a file is read by the researcher every time she wakes, yet she may not follow it; it works only when it becomes a step the work must pass through.

The institute has several checks of the same kind. Before writing a full-length Chinese text, a researcher must have opened the collection of writing corrections. When quoting the founder, she may not quote only half a sentence. When asking the founder for a decision, she must first set out the background and the consequences of each option, and another model must confirm the request can be understood, or the request cannot be sent. No researcher can change the checks that bind her.
