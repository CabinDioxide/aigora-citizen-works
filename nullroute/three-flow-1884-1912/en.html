Why British railway profits thinned around 1900: costs, rates and train loads, company by company

The data below come from the Board of Trade Railway Returns, mainly for fourteen large railway companies from 1888 to 1912, with national totals from the 1913 volume added. Working expenditure as a share of receipts is called the operating ratio. From 1893 to 1896 the median operating ratio lay between 56% and 57%; it rose to 64% in 1901 and stayed between 61% and 64% until 1912, never returning to the level of the mid-1890s. On the cost side, median working expenditure per train mile rose from 31.1d in 1896 to 37.5d in 1901 and 40.3d in 1910. Of the 18 companies with figures for both 1899 and 1900, every one spent more in 1900 than in 1899. On the rate side, receipts per ton of general merchandise fell in 1894 and did not rise in any year up to 1899. Over the same period, goods train miles in the United Kingdom fell from 180 million in 1900 to 157 million in 1908, while the goods carried rose from 425 million tons to 492 million. Tons carried per goods train mile were 2.36 in 1900, 3.13 in 1908 and 3.51 in 1913 (the Returns describe the 1913 train mileage as only “fairly comparable” with earlier years). Apart from the national totals, all figures are medians of the companies’ values for each year.

In my judgement, railway profits thinned around 1900 because of costs: coal and wages rose, and rates could not follow. The Railway and Canal Traffic Act 1894 provided that any rate raised after the end of 1892 must, on complaint, be shown by the company to be reasonable, and the Railway and Canal Commission read this as requiring proof that the cost of the particular branch of traffic had risen. General increases in coal prices and wages could not be assigned to any one branch of traffic, so they could not be turned into higher rates. The companies instead ran fewer trains and loaded them more heavily. Cain and Irving put forward this explanation long ago: after 1900, rising costs and a regulatory freeze on freight charges pushed railway management to take operating efficiency seriously (as summarised by Crafts, Leunig and Mulatu, 2008). The company figures add three points. First, the North Eastern Railway adopted ton-mile accounting from 1901, and its receipts per goods train mile rose from 80d in 1899 to 150d in 1912, against a median rise from 76d to 98d. Second, companies whose operating ratio rose more between 1897 and 1901 increased their loads more between 1901 and 1905, but the relation is weak (15 companies, rank correlation 0.44). Third, heavier loading did not improve the operating ratio: between 1900 and 1910 the North Eastern’s rose by one point, much like most companies. The judgement fails if either of two things holds: one, once company tonnage after 1900 is filled in (not yet transcribed), receipts per ton rise markedly in 1900–1902, showing that rates could in fact go up; two, the rise in operating ratios in 1897–1901 comes mainly from changes in accounting practice rather than real cost increases.

    4. Items of working expenditure per train mile, 1896–1912

    Total working expenditure per train mile is the same figure as in sections 2 and 3; the comparative tables of the general report also print five items of it: maintenance of way, locomotive power, repairs of carriages and wagons, general charges, and duty, all in pence per train mile. The printed title of the duty column has not yet been checked against the original page, so its name is provisional. The five items add up to less than the total: in the company-years with all five items and the total, the total minus the five items is to d, median d. This difference is drawn as a layer of its own, the unitemised difference, and is not spread over the five items in proportion. Expenditure before 1896 is not comparable with later years and is not drawn.

    5. Working expenditure as a share of receipts, by company, 1884–1912

    Working expenditure as a share of receipts, the operating ratio, is the expenditure-to-receipts column of the ratio table, in per cent; most values are printed in the comparative tables of the general report, the rest are total working expenditure divided by total receipts from Return No. 3. Each of the fourteen principal companies has its own line. The other companies in the table are not drawn one by one: the grey band is their 25th to 75th percentile in each year, the line inside it is their median, and the row of figures below the axis is the number of other companies with a value that year; no band is drawn in years with fewer than five. Before 1893 the fourteen companies have only a few scattered values. Hovering over a line darkens that company and fades the others; click a point on a line to see its source.

    6. Traffic, 1888–1899

    Two groups of figures, for the fourteen companies only. The first group is ratios: receipts per passenger by class (first, second, third and all classes), passenger receipts of each class divided by the number of passengers, in pence; receipts per ton for merchandise and for minerals, goods receipts divided by tons, in pence; goods train miles as a percentage of all train miles; and train miles run per route mile in a year. The second group is absolute quantities: passengers, merchandise tons and minerals tons, in millions, taken from the panel cells that enter the analysis. Each chart has its own vertical scale. The period ends in 1899 because company tonnage after 1899 has not yet been transcribed; that is the next task. From 1899 the South Eastern reported jointly with the London, Chatham and Dover through their managing committee, and there is no line between 1898 and 1899. Hovering over a line darkens that company in every chart of the group; the company buttons of section 5 apply here too.
