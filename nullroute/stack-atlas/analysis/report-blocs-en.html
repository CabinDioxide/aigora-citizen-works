If the US and China form Cold War–style opposing blocs, how will supply chains migrate: assembly for the US market has left China, materials processing remains in China, and leading-edge chipmaking equipment remains with the US and its allies

In 2017, 60.2% of the computers the US imported came from China; by 2025 the share had fallen to 4.4%. Over the same period, China's share of US imports of lithium-ion and other storage batteries rose from 24.9% to 47.7%, and of permanent magnets from 61.9% to 72.7%. Both sets of figures come from import records that US customs reports to UN Comtrade. The first shows that supply chains between the US and China have already migrated on a large scale; the second shows that other stages have not left China at all, and that US dependence on China has deepened.

If the US and China form Cold War–style blocs, the question is not whether supply chains migrate, but which stages can migrate and how long each takes. This report sets out the evidence at three levels: trade between countries, company revenue, and the production stages themselves.

My conclusion is that bloc confrontation decides which stages need to move, and the speed of each move depends on the time and cost of rebuilding that stage. Final assembly takes one to a few years to rebuild. This stage has already moved from China to Vietnam, Mexico, Taiwan and India. In 2025 China's share of US direct imports fell to 9.3%, but China's exports to those countries rose in step, and its total exports did not fall. Upstream materials processing is concentrated in China. Stages such as rare-earth magnets, battery materials, graphite and pharmaceutical ingredients would take three to ten years to rebuild, and the main obstacle is China's prices rather than technology; by 2025 US dependence on these products had not fallen. Leading-edge chipmaking equipment and design tools are concentrated in the US and its allies, and China would need five to ten years or more to rebuild them, more than ten for extreme ultraviolet lithography. Before controls tightened, China bought equipment in large quantities; the share of China in the revenue from controlled US chips fell quickly, while Dutch and Japanese equipment makers sold more to China.

So even if bloc confrontation unfolds in full, for the next five to ten years each bloc will still hold stages the other cannot rebuild in the short term. Supply chains will form two systems, but they will be hard to separate completely.

1Countries: China's share of US direct imports fell from 21.9% to 9.3%, and Chinese exports were rerouted to connector countries

China's share of total US goods imports was 21.9% in 2017, 17.1% in 2022 and 13.8% in 2024, and fell to 9.3% in 2025. The fall was largest in 2025, the year the US raised tariffs on China. Electronics fell faster:

Computers need a separate note. US computer imports in 2025 were US$253 billion, nearly double the US$141 billion of 2024, and most of the increase was AI servers assembled in Taiwan and Mexico. Part of the fall in China's share to 4.4% is due to the larger denominator; but in value terms, US computer imports from China also fell from about US$36.7 billion in 2024 to about US$11.1 billion in 2025.

Over the same period China's exports did not fall. China's reported total exports were US$2.26 trillion in 2017, US$3.59 trillion in 2022 and US$3.78 trillion in 2025. Exports to the US fell from US$583 billion in 2022 to US$421 billion in 2025; exports to Vietnam rose from US$72 billion in 2017 to US$199 billion in 2025, to Mexico from US$36 billion to US$89 billion, to Thailand from US$39 billion to US$104 billion, and to India from US$68 billion to US$136 billion. China's exports of integrated circuits rose from US$67 billion to US$203 billion, of which exports to Vietnam rose from US$4 billion to US$27 billion. Vietnam's rising share of US electronics imports and China's rising exports of chips and components to Vietnam are the two ends of the same supply chain.

This matches the findings of existing research. Freund and co-authors at the World Bank find that the countries that replaced China as exporters to the US in 2017–2022 were often already deeply embedded in Chinese supply chains, and their imports from China grew faster; a Dallas Fed study, citing an Asian Development Bank estimate, puts Chinese value added at about 7% of Mexico's exports to the US, and China is now Mexico's largest supplier of motor vehicles. Gopinath and co-authors at the IMF estimate that since the Russia–Ukraine war, trade between countries in different geopolitical blocs has fallen by about 12% relative to trade within blocs, and direct investment by about 20%, less than in the early Cold War. The country-level evidence shows that the final assembly step has left China, but a considerable part of the components and materials used in that assembly still comes from China.

For batteries, permanent magnets, rare-earth compounds and antibiotics, China's share in 2025 was higher than in 2017. Natural graphite is the only item with a clear fall: China's share dropped from 66.4% to 31.1% in 2025, and Indonesia became the second-largest source for the first time, with 26%. Trade data cannot show whose factories produce this Indonesian graphite; company registries would have to be checked to judge whether this counts as Chinese supply chains moving out. Medicaments are a different case: China supplies only 3.9% of US medicament imports directly, and India 16%; but according to this project's node breakdown, a large part of the active ingredients in Indian finished drugs comes from China, a dependence that US import statistics do not show.

This project's node breakdown explains why these stages are hard to move out. IEA statistics show that 94% of the world's sintered NdFeB magnets were produced in China in 2024; China's share of processing of the spherical graphite used in battery anodes is close to all of it; more than 95% of lithium iron phosphate cathode material, more than 90% of solar-grade polysilicon and more than 95% of wafers are in China; and 80% to 90% of penicillin-class active ingredients are in China. The technology for these stages is mostly public; the main obstacle to rebuilding them is price, since China's surplus capacity pushes prices down to a level at which new plants cannot make a profit. The US has begun to respond with government pricing. The Department of Defense signed a ten-year price floor of US$110 per kilogram of NdPr oxide with MP Materials and committed to buy its magnet output. This shows that whether the materials stage can move depends on whether governments are willing to subsidise costs above China's over the long term, not on whether firms are willing to invest.

On 4 April 2025, China's Ministry of Commerce introduced export licensing for seven rare earths: samarium, gadolinium, terbium, dysprosium, lutetium, scandium and yttrium (Announcement No. 18 of 2025). Importers' monthly records show that China can tighten supply within a very short time. Japan's imports of permanent magnets from China were 183 tonnes in May 2025, 45% below the 335 tonnes of May 2024; Korea's were 152 tonnes, 80% below the 750 tonnes of the same month a year earlier. Both countries' imports had returned to the previous year's level by July.

3Equipment: China bought heavily before controls tightened, and the US share of its equipment purchases passed to the Netherlands and Japan

The leading-edge chip stages are concentrated in the other bloc. According to this project's node breakdown, only ASML of the Netherlands can build extreme ultraviolet (EUV) lithography tools in volume, only Carl Zeiss of Germany can supply their projection optics, and a few Japanese companies supply EUV photoresist; three firms, Synopsys, Cadence and Siemens, hold 85% to 90% of chip design software; TSMC holds about 90% of foundry production at 7 nm and below; and NVIDIA holds about 80% of AI accelerators for data centres. The breakdown's estimates of how long China would need to rebuild these stages vary: more than ten years for EUV tools, advanced foundry and the full design-software flow for advanced nodes, where the lithography tool alone requires rebuilding five stages at once — light source, optics, photoresist, pellicle and system integration; five to ten years for NVIDIA's software ecosystem; and three to five years for high-bandwidth memory for a firm that already has an advanced memory process, which is exactly what China lacks.

China's response was to buy as much as possible before controls tightened. China's reported imports of semiconductor manufacturing equipment were US$34.7 billion in 2022, US$47.1 billion in 2024 and US$50.3 billion in 2025. By source:

The four US and Dutch equipment makers all saw China's share of their revenue peak in fiscal 2024 and then decline. In Tokyo Electron's fiscal year to March 2026, revenue from China fell from ¥1.015 trillion the previous year to ¥832.6 billion, while revenue from Korea and Taiwan rose by 33% and 22% respectively. Part of the fall in China's share reflects lower Chinese purchases, and part reflects higher purchases by Korea and Taiwan as they expanded capacity for AI chips.

For chip design companies, the change depends on whether the product is controlled. NVIDIA's data-centre chips have been the main target of US controls since 2022, and China's share of its revenue fell from 26.4% in fiscal 2022 to 9.1% in fiscal 2026. Micron's share fell from 18.2% in fiscal 2021 to 10.1% in fiscal 2025. Qualcomm's phone chips are not controlled in the same way, and China's share of its revenue by shipment destination was still 66.0% in fiscal 2024, almost unchanged from 67.1% in fiscal 2021; Intel's stayed between 24% and 29% over the same period. China's share of TSMC's revenue stayed between 10% and 12% and fell to 8.6% in 2025. For products directly covered by controls, the Chinese market shrank quickly; for products not covered, Chinese assemblers remain the largest customers.

4Future migration: three groups of stages by rebuilding time

Putting this evidence together with the rebuilding times of each node, if the US and China form Cold War–style blocs, the migration of stages falls into three groups.

The first group is already moving, and deeper bloc confrontation will only make the move more complete. The next question is not the assembly plants themselves but whose components the connector countries' plants use. If the US applies rules of origin to Chinese components and materials in Vietnamese and Mexican exports, the next round of migration will happen at the component level. That level requires data on connector countries' imports of intermediate goods from China, which this report did not obtain.

The second group is the slowest to move under bloc confrontation. Technology is not the obstacle; price is. Western capacity can be built only if governments set price floors and commit to buying output over the long term. The Department of Defense's arrangement for rare-earth magnets is the first example, and its effect does not yet show in trade data. Until such capacity is built, China can, as in April 2025, reduce Japan's and Korea's magnet imports by 45% and 80% within one or two months.

The third group cannot move to China's side within five to ten years. What controls can do is keep the newest equipment and chips out of China's hands; what China can do is buy enough previous-generation equipment before controls tighten and expand capacity at mature process nodes.

The last two groups are concentrated in different blocs, and this determines the shape of the confrontation. Between the US and China today, each bloc holds stages the other cannot rebuild in the short term: China cannot do without Dutch lithography tools and American design software, and the US cannot do without Chinese magnets, battery materials and pharmaceutical ingredients. Until these stages are rebuilt, the cost of a full decoupling would fall on the downstream industries of both sides. This is why bloc confrontation is unlikely to be carried through in the short term, and why both sides use controls and licences as bargaining tools.

Several figures should be watched each year from now on: whether China's share of US imports of permanent magnets and storage batteries begins to fall; who owns Indonesia's graphite plants; how China's equipment imports from the Netherlands and Japan change after those countries tighten controls; and China's share of revenue at NVIDIA, Applied Materials and Tokyo Electron in the next fiscal year. A fall in the first two would mean the materials stage has begun to move; changes in the last two would show whether equipment controls are becoming aligned among allies.

•Basis and limits

Country-level trade data are from UN Comtrade, retrieved on 24 September 2026. US imports are as reported by the US; China's exports and equipment imports are as reported by China. US and Chinese records of the same trade often differ, so this report compares figures only within one reporter's data and does not mix the two countries' data. HS codes: storage batteries 8507, metal permanent magnets 850511, rare-earth compounds 2846, antibiotics 2941, natural graphite 250410, medicaments 3004, semiconductor manufacturing equipment 8486, computers 8471, telephones and telecom equipment 8517, integrated circuits 8542, semiconductor devices and PV cells 8541.

Only Japan and Korea are used for monthly magnet data. US and Indian monthly net weights show obvious anomalous jumps (India reported 25,000 tonnes in the single month of August 2024, ten times other months) and are not used. China has reported monthly exports to Comtrade only through 2024, so China's side of the record after the 2025 controls cannot be seen.

Company shares of revenue from China are taken from the tagged data in annual reports filed with the US Securities and Exchange Commission (10-K and 20-F), and from Tokyo Electron's annual securities report filed with Japan's Financial Services Agency through EDINET (document S100YEOO, fiscal year to March 2026, classified by customer location). "China" generally means mainland China; where a company lists Hong Kong separately, it is added in. Most companies assign regions by billing or shipping location, which is not where the chips are finally used; NVIDIA's annual report says so explicitly. From fiscal 2024 Qualcomm discloses regional revenue by customer headquarters (China 46% on that basis); this report uses its separately disclosed share by shipment destination to keep comparability with earlier years; that share for fiscal 2025 was not found in the tagged data.

Node concentration and rebuilding times come from this project's breakdown of 45 nodes. Of these, the 94% for sintered NdFeB is from the text of the IEA's 2025 report and the 99% for gallium from the US Geological Survey; the shares for spherical graphite, LFP cathodes, polysilicon, wafers and pharmaceutical ingredients are mostly relayed industry figures not checked against the original reports; rebuilding times are estimates made in the breakdown, not measurements. The MP Materials price floor is from relayed reports of the company and Department of Defense announcements; the announcement texts were not archived.

Only the abstracts of Gopinath et al. and Freund et al. were read; the Peterson Institute's 2026 policy brief refused access and is not cited; Chinese- and Japanese-language research was not searched in this round.

Two inferences have not been verified: that the fall in China's share of US imports in 2025 is related to that year's tariffs; and that part of the fall in China's share of Tokyo Electron's revenue reflects capacity expansion in Korea and Taiwan. The first can be tested by comparing monthly data with tariff effective dates, the second by reading the management discussion in the companies' annual reports.
