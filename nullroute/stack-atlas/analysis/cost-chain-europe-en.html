After the Hormuz closure, consumers and firms bore most of Europe’s extra energy bill

From March to July 2026 the EU paid €41.8 billion more for crude oil and natural gas imported from outside the EU than in the same months of 2025; at the unit prices of January and February, before the closure, the extra bill is €51.6 billion. Source The money that governments set aside for this shock with stated amounts came to €11.8 billion by 16 June, equal to 23% to 28% of the extra bill. Source The rest passed through market prices to consumers and firms: in August the consumer price of road fuel in the EU was 23.8% higher than a year earlier. Source Taiwan did the opposite over the same period: electricity prices were not adjusted, the treasury carried the difference, and fiscal support exceeded the import increase itself. Source What I want to establish is who bore the cost under each of these two approaches, and how the consequences differ.

My conclusion: Europe’s supply was never interrupted, and the cost was shared through market prices by everyone who uses oil and gas, in proportion to how much they use. Up to July this cost did not produce in manufacturing the feedstock-industry cuts seen in Taiwan; the visible consequences are higher consumer prices and more bankruptcies among transport firms in some countries.

Data retrieved on 21 September 2026: import volumes to June, import values and unit prices and industrial production to July, consumer prices to August, bankruptcy registrations to the second quarter, government measures to 16 June. 1Which supplies were replaced

After the closure, supplies loaded inside the strait all but disappeared from Europe’s imports. Iraqi crude totalled 2.90 million tonnes in March–June, 61.1% less than the 7.45 million tonnes of a year earlier; in June only 0.46 million tonnes were left, probably northern pipeline crude exported through the Turkish port of Ceyhan, which I could not verify with port-level data. Qatari gas still amounted to 0.922 billion cubic metres in March and was zero for three months from April. SourceSource

Saudi Arabia is a different case. Saudi crude totalled 10.33 million tonnes in March–June, 15.7% more than a year earlier, and 3.58 million tonnes arrived in March alone. Source Saudi Arabia has a pipeline from its eastern fields to the Red Sea port of Yanbu, which lies outside the strait; I think most of the Saudi crude Europe received was loaded at Yanbu instead, and this too could not be verified with port-level data. It matches what the Taiwan chain found: Oman’s oil fields are close to Iran, but its loading ports are outside the strait, and its supply to Taiwan rose 34.6%; Kuwait’s and Qatar’s loading ports are inside the strait, and their supply to Taiwan fell 86.5% and 82.1% respectively. Source The ability to substitute was decided by where the loading port lies relative to the strait, not by which country is the supplier.

The gap was filled by producers on the Atlantic. US crude was 41.5% higher in March–June than a year earlier, Guyana 224.8% higher, Norway 12.3% higher. Total crude imports in March–June came to 147.9 million tonnes, 0.7% more than a year earlier; gas imports in June were 8.3% lower than in the same month of the previous year. Europe saw no shortage in quantity. SourceSource

US crude became a substitute source in Europe but not in Taiwan, where supply from the United States actually fell 1.6% over the same period. The Taiwan chain has already established that the spread between Brent and West Texas Intermediate stayed at about 3.1 dollars a barrel before and after the closure, so US crude did not become relatively cheaper because of this shock. Source The difference between the two therefore does not lie in the oil price. I think it lies in freight and voyage length: from the US Gulf of Mexico to Rotterdam takes about two and a half weeks, while reaching Taiwan means going through the Panama Canal or round the Cape of Good Hope, a voyage of more than forty days. This is an inference; I did not obtain freight rates by route for the period to verify it.

Almost all of the increase comes from the price effect on crude oil. The unit value of crude imports was 467 euro a tonne in February and rose to 776 and 786 euro in April and May, 73.6% above the same month of the previous year in May; the strait briefly reopened from 17 June to 8 July, the unit value fell back to 685 euro in June and 582 euro in July, only 19.6% above the same month a year earlier. Once the strait reopened, import unit values began to fall within a month. Source

Liquefied natural gas comes out negative under the first method because European LNG was already expensive in early 2025: in March 2025 the unit value was 903 euro a tonne, higher even than the 2026 peak of 766 euro in May. Taking the unit prices of January and February, before the closure, as the baseline, the extra paid for LNG is €3.55 billion. Each baseline has its justification: the same months of the previous year remove seasonal effects, while the two months before the closure are closer to "the price without this shock". The increase is therefore written as a range, €41.8 to €51.6 billion. Source

3Who bore the cost

For the amounts of government support, Bruegel keeps an item-by-item table that includes only measures with stated amounts. By 16 June European countries had committed €11.83 billion in total. Spain alone accounted for €4.75 billion, about two-fifths; Germany for €1.62 billion, a cut in the energy tax on petrol and diesel for May and June. By type, general cuts in fuel duty or VAT came to €6.22 billion, more than half; support to transport, agriculture, fishing and other sectors €4.02 billion; direct transfers to households €1.59 billion. Measures not targeted at any group made up 54.4%. Source

This money equals 23% to 28% of the extra import bill. Taiwan’s fiscal absorption was US$13.043 billion, of which price subsidies were US$5.690 billion and a capital injection into CPC US$7.354 billion, while Taiwan’s import increase over the same period was US$4.831 billion. Source Taiwan’s money covers more than the import increase of these five months: it also covers earlier losses at CPC and Taipower, so the two ratios cannot be compared directly. But who carried the cost is clear: in Taiwan mainly the treasury, in Europe mainly the users.

Road fuel rose by more than a fifth in every country. Germany cut the energy tax on petrol and diesel by €0.17 a litre for May and June; in August, after the cut had ended, German road fuel was 27.5% above a year earlier, so the cut offset part of the rise for two months only. Household electricity and gas, by contrast, differ widely between countries: Italian electricity rose 16.9%, German electricity fell 5.6%. The German fall began before the war: in January it was already −3.2%, so it is not a response to this shock. French electricity rose only 1.1%, exactly the figure in the column for regulated prices, which shows that France’s regulated tariff kept this cost increase from reaching households; Italy’s regulated energy prices also rose 14.0%, so its regulated prices were raised with costs. Source

The Taiwan chain arrived at one understanding: who bears the cost is decided by pricing rules, not by the market. Europe’s household electricity and gas bear this out. In the same wholesale market and the same month, German household gas fell 2.4% while Belgian gas rose 24.6%; the difference comes from each country’s regulated prices, long-term contracts and adjustment cycles. Road fuel bears it out from the other side: no country regulates the retail price of road fuel, so it rose by more than a fifth everywhere. Source

Annual consumer price inflation in the EU as a whole rose from 2.1% in February to 3.3% in May and was 3.2% in August. Source Taiwan’s rose over the same period from 1.28% to a peak of 2.56%. Source Price increases in the two were similar, but the way the cost was carried differed: Europe’s rise came from energy prices passed straight to consumers; Taiwan’s rise was small because the treasury carried most of the cost.

4Consequences

My original expectation was that with electricity and gas prices following the market in Europe, both energy-intensive industries and industries that use oil and gas as feedstock should cut output. The data do not support that expectation. Chemicals did slightly better after the closure than before it. Refining grew 3.4%, and German refining 13.0%: as margins on refined products widened, European refineries raised their run rates. All three energy-intensive industries grew slightly. Only motor vehicles and pharmaceuticals fell; their declines do not match the timing of energy costs, neither is a feedstock or energy-intensive industry, and the reasons are not pursued here. Source

Over the same period in Taiwan, chemical materials and fertilizers cut output 8.9% and non-metallic minerals 10.6%, while basic metals grew 7.9%; the shock was concentrated in industries that use oil and gas as feedstock. Source Europe shows no such divide, and I think there may be two reasons. First, Europe’s chemical industry already went through a round of contraction in 2022–2025; the most price-sensitive capacity has mostly closed, and what remains is less sensitive to this price rise. Second, Europe’s chemical feedstock comes mainly from the North Sea and the United States, not from the Gulf, whereas a good part of Taiwan’s naphtha is imported from the Gulf. Neither has yet been checked against data. What can be said for certain is that the conjecture that "administered pricing splits an economy into feedstock users and energy users" finds no matching divide in Europe, where prices are not administered. This is consistent with the conjecture, but the European case does not test it, because Europe’s feedstock industries were not hit at all.

Bankruptcy registrations show one change. The EU bankruptcy declaration index in the second quarter of 2026 was 4.5% higher than a year earlier, and 21.0% higher for transport. Transport is among the industries where fuel is the largest share of costs, so the direction fits the rise in oil prices. But country by country it is not uniform: German transport was up 13.1%, France down 5.2%, Italy down 8.2%, Spain down 2.2%. The EU-wide rise comes from countries other than these four, which were not examined one by one here, so it cannot yet be counted as a general consequence of this shock. Source

5Which stage this chain has reached

The state of the European chain is "rerouted and reshaped": substitute sources were found, more money was paid, and the cost was spread out through market prices. Government support covers only a fifth to a quarter or so of the increase, far from any fiscal limit; if the cost keeps accumulating, the first to reach the limit of what they can bear will be households and small firms with a high share of fuel in their costs, not the treasury. The three-week reopening in June gives a reference point: once the strait reopened, crude import unit values fell by about fifteen per cent within a month, so how much further this chain goes depends mainly on how long the strait stays closed. Source

A line is already visible in the feedback-loop cell. Among Europe’s replacement cargoes, Saudi crude is thought to have been loaded on the Red Sea and to have come north through the Suez Canal. Suez Canal revenue in July 2026 was 42% higher than a year earlier, and reports attribute this to tankers diverted through the canal by the Hormuz closure. Source In this way part of the extra freight Europe paid became Egypt’s income. The Egypt chain has its own report; see the Egypt cost page.

Three points are inferences and have not been verified: that Iraq’s remaining crude in June came through the northern pipeline; that most Saudi supply to Europe was loaded at Yanbu instead; and that US crude became a substitute in Europe but not in Taiwan because of freight and voyage length. Port-level loading data could overturn the first two, and freight rates for the same period from the US Gulf to north-west Europe and to East Asia could overturn the third.

The increase covers crude oil and natural gas only, not refined products. A good part of Europe’s diesel and jet fuel is imported from the Gulf, so €41.8 to €51.6 billion is an underestimate.
