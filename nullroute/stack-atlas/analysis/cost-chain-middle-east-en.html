The Middle East after the Hormuz closure: exporters inside the strait lost their income and Iraq can no longer pay salaries; the bypass routes outside the strait came under attack one after another

Before the closure, the Basra oil terminal in southern Iraq loaded about 333 thousand tonnes a day for export; from March to early September 2026 its daily exports were only 0% to 21% of that level. Source Iraq has no other outlet to make up the difference: exports through its northern pipeline to Ceyhan in Turkey ran at 0.85 to 1.14 times the pre-closure level over the same months, with no increase. Oil revenue fell from US$6.81 billion in February to US$1.95 billion in March. Source By the summer the government was spending US$7.77 billion a month against revenue of only US$2.48 billion (estimate: 1.08 + 1.40), Source and in July many public-sector salaries went unpaid. Source The project set out to find a country that could not afford the cost of rerouting, and Iraq is that country: less than five months into the closure it had reached the consequences stage.

My conclusion: how each Middle Eastern state fared in this closure depends on two things. The first is which side of the strait its export ports lie on. The exporters inside the strait (Iraq, Kuwait, Qatar and Bahrain) have all lost most of their export income, and how long each can hold out depends on how thick its pre-war fiscal buffers were. The second is whether the bypass routes can be held. Saudi Arabia and the UAE kept exporting through routes outside the strait, and high prices at first brought them more money, but the routes themselves came under attack one after another: Fujairah in the UAE in March, the Red Sea shipping lane from late July, and Saudi Arabia’s East–West oil pipeline on 10–11 September.

The platform’s data begin only in August 2025, so there is no same period a year earlier to compare with. Source

Two cells to read with care. Yanbu’s 0.03 in August and 0.02 in early September: the platform estimates tonnage from ship position signals and cannot see a ship that has switched its signal off. After the Red Sea attacks began in late July, I judge that Yanbu’s August collapse was mainly ships turning off their position signals to avoid attack rather than a halt in exports, for the reasons given below; September also includes the real stoppage after the East–West pipeline shut on 10–11 September. The same blind spot is why Iran is not in the table: Kharg Island’s pre-closure baseline is only 1.2 thousand t a day, far below its actual exports. Source

Most ports inside the strait are exporting only a tenth to three-tenths of their pre-closure volume: in early September the Basra oil terminal stood at 0.11, Ras Laffan at 0.34 and Sitra at 0.33; Saudi Arabia’s Juaymah was at only 0.04, and Kuwait’s Mina al-Ahmadi has been close to zero since April. Nor did these ports return to their former level during the three weeks from 17 June to 8 July when the strait briefly reopened: Basra reached 0.16 of baseline, Ras Laffan and Sitra 0.21 each, Juaymah 0.18 and Mina al-Ahmadi 0.05. Source

Ports outside the strait fall into two kinds. Yanbu ran at 2.44 to 5.67 times its pre-closure level from March to July, as Saudi exports moved in large part to the Red Sea. Fujairah, though outside the strait, was down to 0.05 in March: the platform recorded 60 tanker calls there in the week to 1 March and only 4 in the week to 8 March, before the drone attack of mid-March; after the attack loadings were suspended, Source then recovered month by month, falling back to 0.10 when the port was hit again in May and reaching 0.70 in early September. The cause of the collapse in the first week of March has not been established here. Source

Yanbu’s August figure needs a separate explanation. The platform recorded 51 tanker calls at Yanbu in the week to 19 July and 18 in the week to 26 July, and only 0 to 2 a week in August, but this does not mean Yanbu stopped exporting. Source In late July the Houthis began attacking shipping at the Bab el-Mandeb, and reports say most tankers leaving Yanbu turned north instead, reaching the Mediterranean through the Suez Canal or Egypt’s Suez–Mediterranean pipeline; Source over the same period Sidi Kerir, that pipeline’s Mediterranean outlet, exported 3.62 times its pre-closure level in August, and its tanker calls rose from 8 to 11 a week in July to 22 in the week to 2 August. Source I think Yanbu’s August collapse was mainly ships switching off their position signals to avoid attack, so that the platform could not see them, rather than a halt in exports.

The real stoppage came in September. On 10–11 September drones launched from Maysan province in Iraq struck the Riyadh and Medina sections of the East–West oil pipeline, and the pipeline was shut; Source Yanbu then suspended loadings. Source Before the closure the pipeline carried 4–5 million barrels a day, the largest single route around Hormuz.

2Exporters inside the strait: how long they can hold out depends on their pre-war buffers

All four countries have lost most of their export income; what separates them is how much buffer each had.

Iraq cannot pay. Almost all its exports leave from Basra. The northern pipeline is built for 1.6 million barrels a day but carries only about 200 thousand, Source and the port data show no increase in exports from Ceyhan. Crude exports were 18.6 million barrels in March against 99.87 million in February, down 81.3%. Source Iraq has not passed a budget for the year; Source in the summer it spent US$7.77 billion a month while oil revenue was 1.08 billion and non-oil revenue 1.40 billion, so revenue covered about a third of spending. Source Many public-sector salaries went unpaid in July, and a finance ministry official said that if the disruption continued salaries could not be paid on time in future; Source a lawmaker has urged the central bank to create money. Source This chain has reached the fifth stage: the cost has landed on the public employees who draw the salaries.

Bahrain is close to not being able to pay. Before the war Bahrain’s fiscal deficit was already 11% of GDP and its public debt 134%, and it needs oil at about US$130 a barrel to balance its budget, the weakest position in the Gulf. Source In March a drone hit the Sitra refinery and force majeure was declared; the refinery accounts for about 70% of government revenue. Source Bahrain is relying on a US$5.4 billion currency swap with the UAE central bank. Source

Kuwait can pay, but it has broken a decades-old rule. Kuwait’s crude output fell from 2.58 million to 0.56 million barrels a day, and it has no alternative export route. Source The deficit for fiscal year 2025/26 reached KWD 7.1 billion, about US$23 billion. Source On 1 September Kuwait for the first time allowed the government to borrow from the Future Generations Fund, with total loans capped at 10% of the fund’s net assets. Source The fund is a sovereign fund set aside for the next generation and in principle never drawn on; this step shows that the ordinary fiscal reserves are no longer enough.

Qatar’s problem is not only the strait. In March Iran struck Ras Laffan and damaged 2 of the 14 LNG trains, about 12.8 million t a year of capacity; repairs will take three to five years, and QatarEnergy estimates the loss at US$20 billion of revenue a year. Source So even if the strait reopens, Qatar’s exports cannot return to their former level. The port data show Ras Laffan recovering to 0.34 of its pre-closure level in early September, Source and reports say August loadings were about 60% below a year earlier. Source

3Countries with bypass routes: the routes became the next target

Saudi Arabia, the UAE and Oman have some or all of their export facilities outside the strait, and in the early months of the closure their position was entirely different from that of the countries inside it. Saudi Arabia ran a record fiscal deficit of US$33.5 billion in the first quarter as war spending surged, Source but its oil revenue in the second quarter was 22% higher than a year earlier, as higher prices outweighed lower volumes. Source All of Oman’s export facilities lie outside the strait; the IMF puts its 2026 growth at 3.7% and expects a budget surplus of 4.5% of GDP. Source

But the bypass routes were not held. Fujairah was attacked twice, in mid-March and in May, Source and UAE output fell by more than half; Source Saudi Arabia’s East–West pipeline was damaged once in April and restored within three days, then shut after being hit again on 10–11 September; Source from late July the Houthis attacked ships in the Red Sea, and on 24 August a Saudi tanker was hit 63 nautical miles west of Yanbu. Source Once one side in the conflict saw which route the cargo had switched to, it attacked that route. In May the UAE said a second pipeline around the strait was half built and would double export capacity through Fujairah; Source this is a buffer built in advance for the next time, but it is just as much within range.

My earlier judgment was that whether a supplier can stand in for others depends on whether its loading ports lie outside the strait. The Middle Eastern data show that this condition is necessary but not sufficient: the loading port must lie outside the strait, and the route must also be held. Fujairah lies outside the strait, yet in March its exports were only 0.05 of the pre-closure level. Source

Sohar in Oman lies outside the strait, yet its exports have stayed at only six- to eight-tenths of the pre-closure level; the reason has not been established here.

4Importers downstream

Jordan used to generate about 60% of its electricity from Israeli gas; after supply was cut at the end of February it switched to burning diesel, Source and generation costs rose by about JOD 100 million a month, about US$140 million. Source Lebanon’s state power company had budgeted its fuel at US$680–690 a tonne; after the crisis began gasoil rose for a time to about US$1500 a tonne, and the cost of 11 gasoil cargoes rose from US$246 million to US$461 million; Source adding war damage and falling bill collection, its losses and extra costs exceed US$410 million, and whether it can keep supplying power to the end of the year is now in doubt. Source

Egypt is covered on the Egypt cost page: it could pay this time, and one reason is precisely that Saudi crude was rerouted through Suez, bringing more revenue to both the canal and the pipeline. 5What this means for the project as a whole

Iraq is the case of a country that cannot pay which the project needed. It went from the closure of the strait to unpaid salaries in less than five months, not because it is much poorer than Kuwait or Qatar but because three things held at once: it had no bypass route that could be expanded, no budget passed, and no large sovereign fund to draw on.

The closure of the East–West pipeline on 10–11 September will change two other chains. Europe imported 15.7% more Saudi crude in March–June than a year earlier; Source most of that oil went north via Yanbu and Suez, and with the pipeline shut this source will be cut. The extra canal and pipeline revenue Egypt earned from rerouted Saudi crude will fall with it. Source After the pipeline attack one analyst said that the buffers that carried the region through the first six months were largely exhausted. The next recalculation of both chains has to start from the September data.
