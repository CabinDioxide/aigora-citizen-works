Six months of the Hormuz closure: Taiwan’s additional import expenditure and who bore it

War broke out on 28 February 2026, the Strait of Hormuz has been closed since early March, and by 19 September that had run 203 days. 61.7% of Taiwan’s crude oil and 33.7% of its liquefied natural gas were loaded inside the strait before the war. Within these six months Taiwan has had no power cuts and no fuel shortage; import costs rose by 40% to 47%, import spending over five months was 4.831 billion US dollars higher than in the same months a year earlier, and most of that has been carried by the budget.

Data to September 2026: imports and output to July, prices and unpaid leave to August, fuel prices and budget items to September. The data sources for each section are listed under the charts and at the foot of the page.

1Transits through the strait have fallen 91%, and have stayed down for 203 days

Daily ship transits through the Strait of Hormuz averaged 74.9 over the twelve months before the war. After Iran announced the closure on 4 March 2026 the average fell to 6.8, a decline of 91.0%. Tankers fell by more: from 41.6 a day to 2.8, a decline of 93.3%. Counted from the outbreak of war on 28 February, 19 September was the 203rd day.

Taiwan imported 295 million barrels of crude oil in 2025, 61.7% of it loaded inside the strait, in Saudi Arabia, Kuwait, the United Arab Emirates, Iraq and Qatar; of the 23.1 million tonnes of liquefied natural gas it imported, Qatar supplied 33.7%.

How this measure is taken

The count of transits comes from the automatic identification system carried by ships. The International Maritime Organization requires vessels of 300 gross tons and above on international voyages to broadcast their position, course, speed and identification number continuously; shore stations and satellites receive those broadcasts, and the ships crossing a line drawn across the strait each day are counted from them. The measure counts ships, not the cargo aboard them. The same cargo can be carried by fewer and larger ships, in which case the count falls while the volume actually moved does not change.

The capacity passing through therefore has to be read alongside the count. Deadweight tonnage is the greatest weight a ship is designed to carry, taken from ship registry records; adding the deadweight tonnage of every ship that passes on a given day gives the upper limit of the capacity that passed that day. Daily deadweight tonnage through the strait fell from 2.84 million tonnes to 200,000 tonnes, a decline of 92.8%, less than two percentage points away from the 91.0% decline in the number of ships. Both measures falling by more than 90% shows that what disappeared was the carrying capacity itself, not that it was repackaged into fewer and larger ships.

There are two things this data cannot see. First, ships that sail with the identification system switched off are not counted, and sanctioned crude oil shipments have long travelled that way, so the transit figure is an underestimate. Second, deadweight tonnage is a ship’s capacity rather than its actual load; an empty ship and a full one enter the same number, so what it measures is capacity rather than cargo.

2Taiwan’s import costs rose a month later than the closure

The average unit price of Taiwan’s crude oil imports was 62.64 US dollars per barrel in February 2026 and 66.89 in March, rose to 96.49 in April, and reached the period’s highest value, 115.23, in May. Liquefied natural gas rose from 467.19 US dollars per tonne in February to 871.47 in April.

What kind of price this unit price is

The import unit price is not a quotation. It is the month’s total import value divided by the quantity imported, an average known as unit value. It mixes long-term contracts, spot purchases and crude oils of different quality together, and so reflects the average cost Taiwan actually paid rather than the price of any single transaction.

Crude oil is recorded free on board. A free-on-board price is the price at the moment the seller loads the goods onto the ship, with freight and insurance borne separately by the buyer, so it records the market at the time of loading rather than at the time of arrival. Liquefied natural gas is recorded cost, insurance and freight, with freight and insurance already inside the price. The two are measured differently and their absolute levels cannot be compared directly.

How long the lag is, and how it can be calculated

The closure took effect at the beginning of March, and international oil prices had passed 100 US dollars per barrel in the first ten days of that month, yet Taiwan’s March import unit price was only 66.89 US dollars, below the 68.63 US dollar weighted average of the twelve months before the war. The reason is that a free-on-board price records the moment of loading: the cargo that arrived in March had been loaded before the war began.

The length of this lag does not have to be estimated; a correlation coefficient can fix it. Align Taiwan’s monthly import unit price with the monthly average price of Brent crude, then shift one against the other month by month and recompute: aligned in the same month the correlation coefficient is 0.705; with Brent leading by one month it rises to 0.965; leading by two months it falls to 0.902; leading by three months, to 0.618. The coefficient takes its maximum at a lead of one month, which means Taiwan’s purchase price lags the international market by about one month on average. That is consistent with a voyage of roughly forty days from the Persian Gulf to Taiwan, because monthly data average away differences within a single month.

The chart shows this directly: in March Brent had risen to 99.41 US dollars while Taiwan was still at 66.89, a gap of 32.52 US dollars; by June Brent had fallen back to 84.49 while Taiwan stood at 112.01, 27.52 US dollars higher in the other direction. Taiwan’s line turns later than Brent’s both on the way up and on the way down.

3Import spending rose by 4.831 billion US dollars, 55% of it from liquefied natural gas

From March to July 2026 Taiwan spent 19.361 billion US dollars in total on imports of crude oil and liquefied natural gas. The same five months of 2025 came to 14.530 billion. The difference between the two is 4.831 billion US dollars.

Why the same months of last year are used for comparison

To calculate how much more was paid, one must first set the amount that would have been paid had there been no closure; that assumed amount is called the counterfactual baseline. Here it is the same five months of 2025. The same months are used rather than a run of months just before the closure because the natural gas Taiwan burns to generate electricity is markedly seasonal: air-conditioning load is high in summer, gas-fired generation rises with it, and imports run above winter and spring levels. Comparing March to July 2026 with October 2025 to February 2026 would count the seasonal difference as part of the effect of the closure.

This choice also has a cost. Between last year’s months and this year’s, things other than the closure changed as well, the most important being that nuclear generation went to zero in May 2025.

How the difference divides into three parts

Spending equals unit price times quantity, and when both move, the change in spending can be written as the sum of three terms: the change in unit price times the original quantity, the change in quantity times the original unit price, and the two changes multiplied by each other. These three add up to exactly the total difference; this is not an approximation.

Price effect +6.189 billion US dollars
The crude oil import unit price rose from 71.40 US dollars per barrel to 100.26, an increase of 40.4%; liquefied natural gas rose from 538.28 US dollars per tonne to 788.41, an increase of 46.5%. Assuming import volumes exactly the same as in the year-earlier months and substituting only this year’s unit prices produces this amount.
Volume effect −0.974 billion US dollars
Crude oil imports were 12.2% lower than in the year-earlier months and liquefied natural gas 3.0% higher. Valued at the year-earlier unit prices, the two together bring spending below the level the price effect implies.
Cross term −0.384 billion US dollars
Unit prices rose at the same time as crude oil import volumes fell, and the imports that did not take place were never charged at the higher unit prices; this amount belongs to neither the price effect nor the volume effect.

Why crude oil imports fell 12.2%

A 12.2% fall in imports admits two explanations: either Taiwan drew down stocks in place of imports while consumption stayed the same, or it genuinely used less oil. Telling them apart requires the refinery intake figure, the quantity of crude oil refineries actually fed into their distillation units. Refinery intake fell 10.1% over the same months, close to the fall in imports, which shows that stocks were not substituted for imports and that the scale of crude oil processing itself contracted.

Why natural gas moved the other way

Liquefied natural gas ran in the opposite direction: its unit price rose 46.5% and import volumes still rose 3.0%. The reason lies in the island’s electricity mix rather than in the import stage. The last nuclear unit shut down in May 2025 and nuclear generation fell from 183.8 billion kilowatt-hours to zero; over the same months hydroelectric generation fell 27.5% and coal-fired generation fell 5.4%. Gas-fired generation filled all three gaps, rising 9.9%. That is to say, Taiwan increased its imports of liquefied natural gas even as the unit price rose 46.5%, because at the time there was no other way of generating electricity available to substitute.

This creates a problem of attribution. Part of the 2.659 billion US dollar difference on liquefied natural gas ought to be recorded against the nuclear phase-out and the fall in hydroelectric generation rather than against the closure of the strait. Separating the two would require knowing how much gas Taiwan would have imported had nuclear power not been withdrawn, and that figure cannot be obtained from the data available. The total of 4.831 billion US dollars is therefore an upper bound on the effect of the closure of the strait, not an exact value.

4The replacement supply came from Oman, not the United States

Supply from countries inside the strait fell sharply after the closure. Against the same five months of 2025, Kuwait’s crude oil deliveries to Taiwan fell from a monthly average of 2.96 million barrels to 0.40 million, a decline of 86.5%, and were zero for four consecutive months from April; Qatar fell from 1.17 million to 0.21 million, a decline of 82.1%; Saudi Arabia fell from 7.51 million to 5.41 million, a decline of 27.9%.

The United States did not take up the shortfall

On the same basis, the United States fell from a monthly average of 7.52 million barrels to 7.39 million, a decline of 1.6%. Over six months of closure, Taiwan bought slightly less crude oil from the United States than in the year-earlier months.

April 2026 was an exception. In that single month Taiwan imported 10.07 million barrels from the United States, the highest value in this nineteen-month series. It lasted one month only: May fell to 7.87 million barrels, June to 4.13 million, July to 5.65 million. The monthly average for the three months from May to July was 5.88 million barrels, below the 6.85 million of the same three months of 2025.

Oman took it up

Oman rose from a monthly average of 2.72 million barrels to 3.66 million, an increase of 34.6%. It supplied nothing to Taiwan in March, then supplied 3.90, 3.99, 5.94 and 4.45 million barrels in four consecutive months from April. Two smaller changes also occurred: the United Arab Emirates rose 8.5%, and Angola rose from zero to a monthly average of 0.77 million barrels, while all remaining sources together rose from 0.50 million to 1.61 million.

Why the United States did not take up the shortfall

Price offers the first test. Crude oil is a commodity traded on a global market, and an interruption of supply in one place raises prices at every source, because cargoes are rerouted until prices in different places again come into line net of freight. The way to test this is the spread between Brent crude and West Texas Intermediate: Brent prices North Sea and Middle Eastern export crude, West Texas Intermediate prices American inland crude, and the difference between them shows how much cheaper American crude is than international crude.

This spread averaged 3.12 US dollars per barrel over the twelve months before the closure and 3.13 over the five months after it, almost no change. Only in the single month of March 2026 did it widen, to 8.03 US dollars; by April it was back down to 1.91, and in June it even turned negative.

From this it can be judged that American crude did not become relatively cheap during the closure. The month in which the spread widened was March, and the month in which Taiwan bought heavily from the United States was the one immediately after, April, which matches a lag of a forty-day voyage plus the accounting convention of one month; as soon as the spread closed, purchases fell back. In other words, the reason for that additional American crude in April was that it could be had at the time, not that it could be had cheaply. Once a loading point outside the strait could be found, there was no longer any ground for bearing the freight of a longer voyage.

What decides whether substitution is possible is the location of the loading port

Oman’s main crude oil loading ports lie on the coast of the Gulf of Oman, outside the strait, and ships leaving them do not have to pass through it; the loading ports of Kuwait and Qatar are both inside. Saudi Arabia falls between the two: its deliveries fell 27.9%, with April at zero and partial recovery from May. Saudi Arabia has a pipeline running from its eastern oil fields to Yanbu on the Red Sea which could in principle avoid the strait, but Taiwan’s import statistics record only the country of origin and not the loading port, so there is no direct evidence for this route.

Two further factors cannot be judged from the data available. The first is freight: the voyage from the US Gulf of Mexico to Taiwan is far longer than the one from the Gulf of Oman to Taiwan and costs more per unit, but month-by-month freight rates for this route were not obtained. The second is crude quality: American exports are mostly light sweet crude and Persian Gulf exports mostly medium sour crude, and refinery units accept feed only within a range of density and sulphur content, which may have limited the quantity of American crude Taiwan’s refineries could take; no public data are available to verify this.

5Pass-through: 22.3% to diesel, zero to the electricity tariff

The cost pass-through rate is the proportion of an increase in input costs that finally shows up in the price charged to the end user. It measures directly who bears the cost first: where the rate is high the consumer bears it; where it is low the bearer is the firm in the middle, or the government.

Crude oil import unit prices rose 40.4% and liquefied natural gas 46.5%. Over the same period the diesel component of consumer prices rose 22.3% and 92-octane unleaded petrol rose 12.4%. The household electricity component rose 4.47%, but that increase comes from a tariff decision of September 2025 and was already in place before the closure; since the closure the electricity tariff has not been adjusted once.

Why the electricity tariff could stay where it was

Taiwan’s electricity price is not set by the market but decided at periodic reviews by the electricity tariff review committee of the Ministry of Economic Affairs, a way of setting prices known as administered pricing. Since the price is produced by a resolution, whether a cost increase passes through to users is a policy choice rather than a market outcome.

The committee resolved on 27 March 2026 that no class of user would be adjusted. The record of its session of 18 September states that “the indicated adjustment for this period is 12.83%”, and resolves to “support giving priority to seeking a government appropriation of 71.1 billion New Taiwan dollars, with no decision on a tariff adjustment on this occasion” (2.236 billion US dollars), leaving the matter to be taken up again in December. That record gives the indicated adjustment, the decision not to make it, and the alternative source of funds together, so a pass-through rate of zero here is not an inference; the resolution itself says so.

Pass-through on fuel prices is also incomplete, and it splits in two

Retail petrol and diesel prices are calculated by the floating oil price formula of CPC Corporation, which takes the previous week’s international oil prices as its input and produces the adjustment due for the current week. CPC’s weekly price announcement divides that indicated adjustment into three parts: the adjustment actually made, the commodity tax the government has reduced, and the portion CPC absorbs itself.

In the week of 19 September 2026 the formula indicated a rise of 0.409 US dollars per litre for 92-octane unleaded, of which 0.116 came from the commodity tax reduction and 0.292 was absorbed by CPC Corporation, so the pump price did not in fact change; for diesel the indicated rise was 0.421 US dollars per litre, with 0.066 from the tax reduction and 0.355 absorbed. From 28 February to 20 September CPC Corporation absorbed 0.689 billion US dollars in total.

Diesel in the consumer price index still rose 22.3%, which shows that the absorption is not total. What CPC absorbs is the portion above a certain threshold; increases below the threshold reach retail prices as usual.

6The share not passed through was borne by the budget: 13.043 billion US dollars in all

The cost was not passed on to consumers and it did not disappear. It landed first on the income statements of two state-owned companies and was then carried by the budget. The supplementary budget bill the Executive Yuan sent to the Legislative Yuan on 3 September 2026 contains four items of expenditure directly related to this increase in costs.

A subsidy and a capital injection are two different things

The first three items are price-gap subsidies: 3.190 billion US dollars to cover what CPC Corporation spent absorbing the part of the oil and gas increase it did not pass on, 2.236 billion to cover Taiwan Power Company’s energy cost gap to the end of August, and 0.264 billion to domestic producers of bottled gas, 5.690 billion in all. All four items are converted from New Taiwan dollars at 31.80, the average rate since March 2026. A subsidy is current government expenditure; it covers directly the shortfall between a company’s selling price and its cost, and enters the company’s income statement.

The fourth item is a capital injection of 7.353 billion US dollars into CPC Corporation, equivalent to 7.354 billion US dollars. A capital injection is not expenditure but a government subscription of equity; it enters the balance sheet rather than the income statement. The two together come to 13.044 billion US dollars.

That a capital injection was needed shows the problem is no longer one of current losses alone. The reason the Ministry of Economic Affairs gave is that CPC’s “debt ratio now stands as high as 93%”: the company has been covering the gap between oil and gas costs and prices by borrowing, and once the ratio of debt to assets reached 93% its capacity to borrow further was close to exhausted, leaving the shareholder to supply equity instead. At Taiwan Power Company, the company’s statement of 4 September records that “as of July it had already lost 21.4 billion New Taiwan dollars, with accumulated losses reaching 372.2 billion”, that is 0.673 billion US dollars for the period and 11.704 billion accumulated, against a profit of 0.129 billion US dollars in the year-earlier period.

One further channel is not counted in this total

The commodity tax reduction is a fourth channel. Its nature is tax the government does not collect, which in public finance is called a tax expenditure: its effect is the same as a subsidy, but because it does not pass through an expenditure line it does not appear among the four items of the supplementary budget. The 13.043 billion US dollar total above does not include it, so the actual fiscal cost is higher than that figure. By how much depends on the volume of fuel sold while the reduction was in force, which has not been calculated.

7The cuts in output are concentrated in industries that use oil and gas as raw material

From March to July 2026 the three industries whose production index fell most against a year earlier were non-metallic mineral products, that is cement and glass, at −10.6%; chemical materials and fertilisers at −8.9%; and petroleum and coal products, that is refining, at −8.2%. The largest increases over the same months were computers, electronic and optical products at +80.0% and electronic parts and components at +19.8%.

The production index measures output, not the value of output

The production index takes 2021 as its base period and is computed from the physical output of each product weighted by fixed weights, so it is not affected by price movements. That matters here: with value of output, the figures for the chemical industry would be inflated by the higher prices of its products and the fall in output would not be visible.

What separates these two groups is not electricity consumption

The industries that cut output use oil and natural gas as raw material, not as energy. Cement and glass kilns have to run continuously above 1,400 degrees Celsius with the fuel fed straight into the kiln; the ammonia synthesis behind fertiliser takes natural gas as its raw material, the gas molecules themselves entering the product; petrochemical ethylene and propylene come from cracking naphtha, and naphtha is a fraction of refined crude oil. These three kinds of input are bought at international prices, administered pricing does not reach them, higher costs compress profit directly, and the response of the firms is to cut output.

The industries that increased output use oil and natural gas as energy, and indirectly at that: what they buy is electricity, supplied by Taiwan Power Company at an administered price, and that price was not adjusted. The cost of the closure therefore did not reach their production costs.

One piece of corroborating evidence: Formosa Petrochemical and Formosa Plastics both declared force majeure on petrochemical feedstock on 10 March 2026, cut output in their petrochemical businesses to an operating rate of about 50%, and suspended acceptance of spot orders from April; Formosa Plastics extended the declaration to polyvinyl chloride, polyethylene and polypropylene. Force majeure is a contractual clause invoked by a party that cannot perform because of an uncontrollable event, releasing it from liability for breach; invoking it means the company judged that the interruption to its feedstock supply had gone beyond normal business risk.

The 80% in computers and electronics was not caused by this shock

Computers, electronic and optical products grew 80%, far more than any other industry. This figure has no causal connection with the closure of the strait; it comes from demand for artificial intelligence servers. It is set out here because it explains why the production index for manufacturing as a whole still rose while the petrochemical industries were cutting output by about 9%: one industry can carry enough weight to pull the total up.

8Consumer prices peaked at 2.56%, and almost all of the rise came from fuel

The change in the consumer price index against a year earlier averaged 1.28% over the six months before the closure, reached a peak of 2.56% in June 2026, and fell back to 2.04% in August.

Why it rose only this much

The consumer price index assigns weights to categories of goods according to the composition of household spending. Vehicle fuel carries a weight of about 4% in Taiwan, and electricity and gas together less than 4%. So even a 20% rise in fuel prices contributes only about 0.8 percentage points directly to the overall index.

The actual decomposition matches that order of magnitude. The vehicle fuel component rose from −7.64% against a year earlier in February 2026 to +20.09% in May, and stood at +11.86% in August; the electricity and piped gas components did not move at all over the whole period. The overall index rose from 1.28% to 2.56%, an increase of 1.28 percentage points, almost all of it from fuel alone.

The electricity component stood at 4.47% against a year earlier in August. That increase comes from the tariff decision of September 2025, was in place before the closure, and has nothing to do with this episode. Note that February 2026 cannot be compared directly with July: residential electricity in Taiwan is charged at summer rates from June to September, so the electricity component jumps about 22% in those months every year, which is a seasonal pricing arrangement and not a tariff change.

This also shows something else: a price index that looks mild does not mean the cost of this shock was small. Most of the cost did not travel by way of consumer prices. It landed on company income statements and on the government budget, and neither of those enters the price index.

9Public reaction ran at about a quarter of the strength of the 2022 blackout

Over a window running from January 2022 to September 2026, three search terms share a single normalisation. During the island-wide blackout of March 2022 the term “power outage” reached 100, the highest value in the window. The highest value during this closure is “fuel price” at 26 in March 2026, about a quarter of the former. “Electricity price” did not exceed 5 at any point.

What this measure captures is relative attention

Google Trends gives not a number of searches but a relative value: the weekly search volume for a term is first divided by all searches in that week to give a ratio, the highest ratio within the window is then set to 100, and the remaining weeks are scaled in proportion. So it can compare high and low points within one window but cannot give an absolute number of people. Placing the three search terms under a single normalisation is what makes them comparable with one another, and that is why this chart uses a shared scale rather than normalising each term separately.

It has two limits. First, it reflects only the population that searches, and does not stand for the public as a whole. Second, the choice of terms affects the result, and a different set of words might produce a different ranking. The three chosen here cover concern about fuel prices, electricity prices and power cuts, but other ways of expressing the same concern may well have gone uncounted.

The action that actually occurred

Collective action occurred once: on 20 May 2026 about a hundred taxis in Kaohsiung surrounded the Kaohsiung city government to petition, their demands including a revision of fares. Separately, on 17 June the mayor of Kaohsiung asked the central government, at a fishery association members’ meeting, to increase its support for fishermen, fuel costs making up 30% to 40% of the operating costs of fishing. There was no national protest.

The number of workers on unpaid leave did not rise. The Ministry of Labor’s statistics on reduced working hours fell from 10,991 people in early March 2026 to 7,265 in mid-September, a decline of a third. These statistics cover only establishments that report to the Ministry of Labor, with those that do not report left out, so the figure is a lower bound.

Street demonstrations did take place in other countries over the same period. Public Television Service reported on 16 September that French fishermen were blockading fuel depots by burning pallets and trucks, that people in Guatemala were burning cars to demand subsidies after diesel rose nearly 70% in half a year, and that residents of Aleppo in Syria were burning tyres to block roads. What these countries have in common is a government that either could not carry the price gap or chose not to.

A weak reaction does not mean there were no political consequences

Energy policy has entered the political agenda. On 14 August the Legislative Yuan voted to pass national referendum proposal 22, putting to a popular vote whether to support the use of nuclear power and thereby repeal the non-nuclear homeland policy; the vote is set for 28 November, the same day as the local elections. During this shock gas-fired generation filled the gap left by the nuclear phase-out, and filled it while gas prices were rising 46.5%. That fact has entered the referendum debate.

10What remains undetermined

  • Within the 2.659 billion US dollar difference on liquefied natural gas, the proportion that should be attributed to the nuclear exit and to lower hydroelectric generation cannot be separated out with the data available.
  • There is no direct evidence for the route by which Saudi supply went to zero in April and partly recovered from May. Rerouting through the east-west pipeline to the Red Sea is one possibility and has not been verified.
  • August import data are released on 2 October, so the full six-month amount cannot be calculated yet. Estimated at the July level, the six-month difference would be in the region of 5.5 billion US dollars.
  • The break-detection rule used in this dashboard gives a date of 18 March 2026, eighteen days later than the actual closure. The rule requires the 28-day rolling mean to fall below half the preceding 180-day mean and to hold there for 14 consecutive days, which lags any abrupt interruption systematically.
  • Monthly profit and loss figures for CPC Corporation and Taiwan Power Company could not be obtained. Both companies delete the previous version of a statement once a new one is published, and the Internet Archive holds no copy, so only the cumulative figures given in company announcements can be used.
