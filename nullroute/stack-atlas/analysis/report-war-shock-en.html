How large was the shock from the Russia–Ukraine war and the Middle East turmoil to global supply chains: very large at the straits and for a few commodities, very small in the global aggregate, with the losses borne mainly by the importers most dependent on the Gulf

In the thirty days from 22 August to 20 September 2026, the deadweight tonnage of ships passing through the Strait of Hormuz was 97% below its daily average in the six months before the blockade. Over the same period, the combined transit tonnage of the world's 28 main straits and canals was only 2.4% below its pre-blockade level. Both figures come from the IMF's PortWatch platform, and they describe different things: Hormuz almost stopped, yet global shipping as a whole barely declined. Existing research and reports from international organisations mostly cite only one of the two, so some stress that "nine-tenths was cut off" while others stress that "world trade is still growing". To judge how large the shock was, one also has to ask which commodities were cut off, what share of world supply they had represented, and whether importers ended up receiving less, or switched suppliers and paid more.

My conclusion is that the shock from the two wars to global supply chains was concentrated on a few routes and a few commodities, and there it was very large; world trade in aggregate changed little, because fleets changed routes, importers changed suppliers, and trade in goods related to artificial intelligence grew quickly over the same period. The real differences lie between importing countries. The deeper a country's dependence on the Gulf, the more its imports of crude oil and liquefied natural gas fell: 95% of Japan's crude came from the Gulf, and its crude imports from March to June 2026 were 25% lower than a year earlier; only 7% of Germany's crude came from the Gulf, and its import volume actually rose by 3%, while its import bill rose by 56%. By 2024, Russia was still exporting crude oil, wheat and potash; only the buyers had changed, to India, China, Turkey and Egypt. What actually disappeared from world supply was Ukraine's steel billets and neon, and, since August 2026, the exports of the Sea of Azov ports.

Iran declared Hormuz closed in early March. PortWatch shows that from 18 March the tonnage passing the strait was close to zero, except for three weeks from 17 June to 8 July when it reopened under the Islamabad Memorandum. UNCTAD's April report, using Clarksons ship data, counts an average of 129 transits a day through Hormuz from 1 to 27 February and 6 a day from 1 to 29 March, a fall of 95%, close to the PortWatch result. The Kerch Strait still saw more than 330 ships a month in May and June, but only 86 in July, 5 in August and 1 from 1 to 20 September, so exports from the Sea of Azov ports have essentially stopped. Transit through the Bosporus has also fallen. Tanker tonnage through the Strait of Malacca is down 36%, consistent with Gulf crude no longer moving east, since most tankers from the Gulf to East Asia used to pass Malacca.

Traffic rose through several passages around the Philippines and through the Tsugaru Strait. The Luzon Strait is up 45% and the Mindoro Strait 80%, mostly container ships and tankers. My inference is that, after East Asian importers turned to Atlantic-side suppliers, some ships entering the Indian Ocean from the Cape of Good Hope sail north through eastern Indonesia and the Philippine passages instead of through Malacca. This has not been checked ship by ship.

The "total of 28 routes" adds up the transit tonnage of each route. A ship passing two routes is counted twice, so the figure is not world freight; it only shows that the world fleet has not stopped as a whole but is taking other routes. Most of the roughly 2.7 million dwt lost at Hormuz and 1.6 million dwt lost at Malacca reappears on other routes.

By value, crude oil is the largest item: the eight Gulf states supplied US$482.2 billion of it in 2024. Oman's loading ports lie outside the strait, and Saudi Arabia and the UAE each have a pipeline to a port outside it, so these shares are upper bounds on what was affected, not the quantities actually cut off. Supply is most concentrated for sulphur, methanol, urea and rare gases. These are not energy as such but inputs for chemicals and semiconductors: methanol and sulphur are chemical feedstocks, urea is a nitrogen fertiliser, and helium is a process gas in wafer fabs.

The Gulf states do not report exports to UN Comtrade, so the change in their actual exports after the blockade can only be taken from estimates by international organisations. The International Trade Centre estimates that the Gulf states' combined export volume in April 2026 was 54% below April 2025, with LNG down 95%, urea 83%, methanol 80%, ammonia 75%, and export value down 21%. WTO statistics show that in March world imports from the Middle East fell by 45% for crude oil, 52% for LNG and 26% for fertilisers. Export values fell less than export volumes because prices rose.

The World Bank's monthly price table shows the price changes. Brent crude averaged US$69.0 a barrel in 2025, reached US$107.5 in May 2026 and fell back to US$83.4 in July. Middle East f.o.b. urea averaged US$422.7 a tonne in 2025, was US$770.5 in May and US$400.0 in July. European natural gas averaged US$11.96 per million Btu in 2025 and was US$18.06 in July, above its May peak. Aluminium averaged US$2,632 a tonne in 2025, was US$3,666 in May and US$3,161 in July. Crude and urea prices fell after the strait reopened in late June and did not return to their May highs when it closed again in July; European gas went the other way, with its July price above May.

The six crude-oil and LNG observations show that the deeper the dependence on the Gulf, the more total import volume fell. Japan's and India's crude imports both fell by about a quarter, and India's LNG imports by a fifth. Germany and the US hardly depend on Gulf crude. When their small Gulf supplies fell, other sources made up the gap, so their import volumes did not fall; their import bills rose by 56% and 20% respectively. Only 11% of Japan's LNG comes from the Gulf, mainly from Australia and Southeast Asia, so its import volume rose rather than fell.

India's urea is a different case. 72% of India's urea imports came from the Gulf. After the blockade India's imports from the Gulf fell by 39%, but it bought more from other sources, so its total import volume rose by 23% and its import bill was four times that of a year earlier. My inference is that the Indian government bought enough fertiliser before the summer sowing season regardless of price. Dependence alone does not determine the outcome. Whether an importer ends up with less or pays more also depends on how high a price it is willing to pay.

Several earlier analyses in this project calculated the additional spending of some importers. From March to July, the EU paid €41.8 billion more than a year earlier for crude oil and natural gas imported from outside the Union; government allocations covered 23% to 28% of this, and consumers and firms bore the rest in proportion to their use. Taiwan's additional import spending over the same period was borne by the treasury, and electricity prices were not adjusted. Egypt's natural gas imports cost about US$1.1 billion more each month, spread through fuel price increases, depreciation of the Egyptian pound and IMF disbursements. Exporters' losses were also concentrated: by 18 September, export revenue from Iraq's southern ports was down by about US$30.7 billion, Qatar's by US$12.5 billion and Kuwait's by US$6.7 billion, and since July the Iraqi government has not paid public employees on time.

Russia's shares of wheat, potash, sunflower oil and nitrogen fertilisers are all higher than before the war, and its crude share fell by only 1.3 percentage points. Sanctions changed Russia's buyers. From 2021 to 2024, India's imports of Russian crude rose by US$50.4 billion and China's by US$22.1 billion, while the Netherlands' fell by US$9.5 billion, Poland's by US$6.6 billion, the US's by US$4.8 billion and Korea's by US$4.3 billion. More Russian refined products went to Turkey, China, Brazil and Singapore, while France, Korea, Germany and the UK bought less. More Russian wheat went to Egypt, Algeria and Saudi Arabia. In platinum-group metals, the US, Germany, Japan and Italy together imported about US$4.7 billion less, while Armenia imported US$0.43 billion more over the same period, consistent with what researchers at the EBRD describe as re-export through Central Asia and the Caucasus.

What actually fell in world supply was part of Ukraine's exports. Ukraine's share of world steel billets fell from 10.0% to 3.3% and of barley from 12.3% to 7.3%, and its shares of nitrogen fertilisers and of neon-containing rare gases fell almost to zero. Its maize share instead rose from 9.7% to 13.4%, showing that its grain export route was still working in 2024.

The situation changed in 2026. The Kerch Strait has been almost closed since August, and exports from the Sea of Azov ports have essentially stopped. The project's ship monitoring recorded not a single port call at the three Odesa ports during its four-hundred-day observation window, so Ukrainian grain can leave only via the Danube. The number of ships waiting to enter the Danube at its mouth rose from 10 to 43 within two weeks in mid-August. Even so, Egypt imported 4.35 million tonnes of wheat from March to June 2026, 36% more than a year earlier, including 2.55 million tonnes from Russia, up 34%, and 1.02 million tonnes from Ukraine, up 31%. Black Sea grain was still flowing out this summer, but the routes open to it were becoming fewer.

5The global aggregate: trade volumes are still growing, and prices peaked in May

According to WTO statistics, world merchandise trade volume in the first quarter of 2026 was 3.2% higher than a year earlier; Middle East export volume fell by 9.7% and import volume by 11.9%, while trade in AI-related goods rose by more than 40% in value. The WTO projects world trade volume growth of 1.9% for 2026, or 1.4% under a high-energy-price scenario, against 4.6% in 2025; UNCTAD's April forecast puts 2026 real merchandise trade growth at 1.5% to 2.5%, against 4.7% in 2025. The September Goods Trade Barometer stood at 102.0, still above the trend value of 100. The New York Fed's Global Supply Chain Pressure Index is measured in standard deviations above its long-run average. It was close to zero in most months of 2025, rose to 0.58 in December and was 0.59 in February, before the war began; it jumped to 1.85 in April, the highest of this episode, was 1.83 in May, then fell back to 1.20 in June and 0.94 in July, and rose again to 1.06 in August. The index was already elevated before the war, consistent with its authors' explanation: in a May article they attribute the rise to longer delivery times and order backlogs, partly driven by equipment demand from AI data centres.

World trade in aggregate did not fall because of the two wars, but this does not mean the shock was small. The WTO attributes part of the aggregate growth to demand for AI goods: in the same quarter, trade in one category of goods grew quickly while another was cut off, and the sum still looked fairly stable. UNCTAD's June report notes that after the announcement on 14 June that the strait would reopen, crude prices fell but the freight index for grains and oilseeds stayed high; its judgement is that energy prices can adjust quickly while shipping and value chains take longer to recover.

The shock to routes and commodities was very large. The eight Gulf states affected by the Hormuz closure had supplied 36.3% of world crude imports, 35.2% of urea, 42.3% of methanol and 33.1% of rare gases; their exports fell by more than half in April. Brent's average price in May was 56% above its 2025 average.

The shock borne by an importer depended on its dependence on the Gulf and on the price it was willing to pay for replacement supplies. Germany and the US, with little dependence on Gulf crude, kept their import volumes, and the additional cost was shared among consumers, firms and public budgets. Japan and India, with deeper dependence, saw their actual imports of crude and LNG fall. Three years after the Russia–Ukraine war began, most of Russia's exports had found new buyers, and Ukraine's exports of steel, chemicals and port services suffered greater losses.

The change in the global aggregate was small. The combined transit of the 28 routes fell by 2.4%, trade volumes kept growing, and oil prices had already fallen back by July. Two things kept the aggregate up: fleets and supplies could be rerouted and replaced, and demand for AI-related goods grew over the same period. The first reflects the ability of supply chains to adjust; the second has nothing to do with the two wars.

Three indicators are worth comparing every month from now on: whether the monthly crude and LNG imports of Japan, India and, once it reports, Korea return to last year's levels; whether European gas prices keep rising after July; and the number of ships waiting at the mouth of the Danube. These indicators can show early whether the shock is fading or moving elsewhere.

•Basis and limits

Route data are PortWatch's daily transit records (IMF), up to 20 September. "Deadweight tonnage" is the total deadweight tonnage of ships passing the route on a given day. The pre-blockade baseline is 1 September 2025 to 28 February 2026; PortWatch data begin on 20 August 2025, so only this one thirty-day window can be compared with the previous year.

Trade data are from UN Comtrade, retrieved on 24 September 2026. Importer-reported figures are used throughout, because the Gulf states, Iran and Russia have in recent years mostly not reported exports. Import values include freight and insurance and are a few percent higher than the f.o.b. values reported by exporters. The numerator and denominator of each share use the same set of reporting countries; a reporter that gives a world total but no Gulf partner rows is counted as importing nothing from the Gulf.

In the monthly comparison, only Japan, India, Germany, Egypt and the US had reported through June 2026; Korea, China and Turkey had no 2026 monthly data yet. The relation between dependence and the fall in import volume therefore rests on six observations and shows only a direction; it cannot estimate an elasticity. Three monthly series could not be used: Germany's urea lacks net weight; the US's previous-year imports of rare gases from the Gulf were only a few hundred thousand dollars, so the growth rate is meaningless; and India's aluminium net weight fell 56% while its value rose 13%, inconsistent with aluminium prices over the period and probably an unreported net weight. None of the three is used in the text.

Prices are from the World Bank Commodity Price Data (Pink Sheet), 4 August 2026 edition. Urea is the Middle East f.o.b. spot price; LNG is the Japan c.i.f. import price.

The ITC figures on Gulf export volume in April come from the UN News report of 4 August 2026 on that analysis; the original report was not read. WTO figures are from its press releases of March, July and September 2026 and its fertiliser data blog of 10 July. Monthly values of the Supply Chain Pressure Index are from the index data file published by the New York Fed, downloaded on 24 September 2026, with data to August; the authors' explanation is from a May 2026 Liberty Street Economics article by the New York Fed. Both UNCTAD reports on Hormuz, of 1 April and 30 June 2026, were read in full. The claim circulating in search summaries that "about a third of the world's helium and half of its urea pass through the strait" appears in neither report and is not cited here.

The additional spending and cost-bearing of the EU, Taiwan, Egypt and the Middle Eastern states come from four earlier analyses in this project, on Europe, Taiwan, Egypt and the Middle East. The situation at Odesa and the Danube mouth comes from the project's theatre conclusions of 23 September. Those conclusions originally stated that no ship had passed the Kerch Strait since 30 April; this was wrong: 30 April is the date on which the monitoring program detected the start of a decline in traffic, and PortWatch's daily records show more than 330 ships a month still passing in May and June. The Kerch figures here follow the daily records.

Two inferences have not been verified: that the rise in traffic through the passages around the Philippines comes from route changes after East Asian importers turned to Atlantic-side suppliers; and that the rise in India's urea imports reflects a government decision to buy enough fertiliser before the summer sowing season regardless of price. Ship-by-ship tracks could test the first, and the procurement tender records of India's Department of Fertilizers the second.
