Egypt could pay this time: fuel price rises and a weaker pound spread the cost over all residents, and IMF money, pre-war contracts and canal revenue filled the currency gap

When the war began on 28 February 2026, Egypt lost two things at once: Israeli gas delivered by pipeline (still 0.802 billion cubic metres in January) stopped that day, Source and the closure of the Strait of Hormuz raised the price of the liquefied natural gas and refined fuel it now had to buy instead. According to Prime Minister Madbouly in March, gas imports therefore cost US$1.1 billion more each month; Source by an Israeli media calculation, Egypt’s monthly energy import bill rose from US$0.56 billion to US$1.65 billion. Source The project plan set out to find a country that could not afford the cost of rerouting, and Egypt was one of the candidates. I wanted to establish whether Egypt could in fact pay, and if not, where the trouble would show first.

My conclusion: up to August, Egypt could pay. The cost was spread over all residents by two means: a one-step fuel price rise of 14% to 30% on 10 March, and a 14.4% fall of the pound against the dollar in six weeks. The currency gap was filled by International Monetary Fund disbursements, oil hedges and long-term gas contracts signed before the war, and the extra Suez Canal revenue brought by the Hormuz closure itself. None of the three was prepared for this occasion.

1What Egypt lost

More than four-fifths of Egypt’s electricity comes from gas, and Israeli pipeline gas was one of its main sources of imported gas. On 28 February Israel shut the Leviathan and Karish fields in the north and kept the output of the Tamar field for its own market; exports to Egypt and Jordan stopped immediately. By 21 September no date for the resumption of supply could be found. Source

The gap could only be filled with liquefied natural gas. With Hormuz closed, Qatari LNG could not leave the strait and world LNG prices rose; Egypt had to make up the volume once supplied by pipeline in a rising market. Its energy import bill therefore rose by about US$1.1 billion a month. Source In the budget for fiscal year 2026/27 (from July), the allocation for gas and LNG imports is US$10.7 billion, 26% more than the previous year; Source the allocation for gasoline, diesel and liquefied petroleum gas imports is US$5.5 billion, almost two-fifths more. Source

2Who bore the cost

Egypt differs from Taiwan in two respects.

First, retail fuel prices were raised in a single step, and consumers bore the cost directly. In the early hours of 10 March the petroleum ministry raised gasoline and diesel by EGP 3 a litre each: 95-octane gasoline went from EGP 21 to EGP 24, up 14.3%; diesel from EGP 17.5 to EGP 20.5, up 17.1%; vehicle natural gas from EGP 10 to EGP 13 per cubic metre, up 30%. Source In October 2025 Egypt’s automatic fuel pricing committee had just announced a one-year price freeze; on the tenth day of the war, with oil above 115 dollars a barrel, the pledge was dropped. In Taiwan over the same period the pass-through rate was 12.4% for gasoline and 22.3% for diesel, reached through adjustments over several months; Source Egypt made its adjustment in one day.

Second, the pound fell, spreading import costs over every imported good. On 16 February the pound stood at 46.68 to the dollar and on 31 March at 54.53; its dollar value fell 14.4% in six weeks. Source Every import besides fuel, including the wheat Egypt imports in large quantities, became dearer in pounds as a result. By mid-September the rate was back to about 50.95, but not to its pre-war level. Source

Household electricity was the exception. The government denied raising household tariffs and said there would be no load shedding in summer. The cost of gas for power generation rose, household tariffs did not follow, and the difference was carried by the state power company and the budget; the amount could not be found. Source

Annual urban consumer price inflation was 13.4% in February; after the fuel price rise and the fall of the pound it rose to 15.2% in March, then eased month by month to 14.5% in August. After a rise of 1.8 percentage points (estimate: 15.2 − 13.4) it did not climb further. SourceSourceSource

3What filled the currency gap

On 30 July the International Monetary Fund completed its seventh review of its lending arrangement with Egypt and disbursed about US$1.8 billion. The IMF press release states that the impact of the shock was limited because of record remittances, resilient tourism receipts, a recovery in Suez Canal revenue, oil hedging contracts and long-term gas supply agreements. Source

None of these buffers was prepared for this war. The IMF arrangement dates from 2022. The press release does not say which "long-term gas agreements" it means; in 2025 Egypt signed a batch of long-term LNG purchase agreements, and in June of that year Israel had also interrupted gas supply to Egypt, so I think the press release mainly refers to that batch, which I have not verified. When the oil hedges were signed and how large they are could not be found. The Taiwan chain found the same thing: Taiwan could switch gas sources thanks to a third receiving terminal commissioned ten months before the crisis. Source The buffers available in a shock were often built earlier for other reasons, and Egypt is the second example.

Canal revenue is the most unusual item in this chain. After the Hormuz closure, tankers sailing to Europe from producers outside the Gulf switched to the Red Sea and the Suez Canal; canal revenue in the second quarter of 2026 was US$1.26 billion, 13% more than a year earlier, Source and in July it was US$0.505 billion, 42% more than in the same month of the previous year, with 1340 ships passing that month. Source On the July figure, the canal earned about US$0.15 billion more than a year earlier (estimate: 505 − 505 ÷ 1.42, in US$ million), against about US$1.1 billion more paid for energy imports each month. The extra canal revenue offset about a seventh of the extra energy bill, not enough to close the gap, but running the other way: the same closure made Egypt pay more in one place and earn more in another.

4Which stage this chain has reached, and where trouble would show first

At the end of August Egypt’s international reserves were US$57.214 billion, a record high and US$0.921 billion more than in July (estimate: 57214 − 56293, in US$ million). All of the increase came from gold: gold holdings rose from US$17.139 billion to US$19.058 billion, while foreign-currency holdings fell by US$1.158 billion that month. The rise in reserves reflects a higher gold price, not larger foreign-currency inflows. Source

I therefore think the first place this chain will show trouble is foreign-currency holdings. Egypt has to pay about US$1.1 billion more in foreign currency for energy imports each month; the extra canal revenue offsets only about US$0.15 billion (estimate, see above), and the rest depends on remittances, tourism, IMF disbursements and existing reserves. If foreign-currency holdings keep falling for several months as they did in August while the strait stays closed, Egypt’s next options are another fuel price rise, a further fall of the pound, or cutting import volumes; the first two would push inflation up again, and the third would mean this chain moving from "rerouted and reshaped" to "rationed".

Egypt could pay this time, so it is not yet the "cannot pay" case the project needs. The next candidate should be an energy importer without an IMF arrangement, with thin reserves, and without an unexpected source of income like the canal.
