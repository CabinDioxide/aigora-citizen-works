From 1894 Parliament set the profit margin of British railways: receipts could not rise, costs were raised by law, and expenditure as a share of receipts went from 57% to 63% and never came back

This weave shows where each penny earned per train mile by fourteen British railway companies went, year by year from 1888 to 1912. It establishes one fact: from 1894 these companies no longer set their own profit margin. The maximum rate schedules took effect in January 1893, and the Railway and Canal Traffic Act 1894 required a company to justify any increase in charges. Receipts per train mile fell from 64d in 1889–1891 to 57d in 1893–1898 and did not regain 64d until 1911. Over the same years costs were raised by law: the Workmen's Compensation Act took effect in 1898 and the Railway Employment (Prevention of Accidents) Act in 1900, and expenditure per train mile rose from 31d in 1896 to 38d in 1901 and 40d in 1910, with 18 of 20 companies turning upward together in 1900. Taken together, expenditure as a share of receipts rose from 56–57% in 1893–1896 to 62–64% in 1900–1901 and stayed between 60% and 64% through 1912.

My judgement: the decline of the private British railway did not begin with motor transport; it began in the six years 1894–1900 in which Parliament fixed prices and raised costs at the same time. The Act of 1913 permitting increases and the grouping of 1921 were both remedies for that period. The invisible institution this project set out to find is the Act of 1894: not a word of it says that prices are frozen, and it froze them for nearly twenty years. The figure shows the same thing company by company: drag the year slider, or click 1893, 1894, 1898 or 1900, and from 1896 the grey strand (working expenditure) inside each company's receipts band widens year by year while the blue strand (net receipts) narrows, and after 1900 it does not recover. The figures above are medians of the companies' values for each year; the band widths are each company's own values.

    Where the figures come from

    Two printed values per company per year: total receipts per train mile and total expenditure per train mile as printed in the Board of Trade railway returns, in pence, taken from the two matching rows of the ratio table in the task 010 return box; net receipts is their difference, not a ratio multiplied by receipts. The companies are the fourteen of Sigma's twelve-year weave (South Eastern & Chatham is reported as one managing committee from 1899; before that South Eastern reports alone). Where one company, year and ratio had several rows, the row printed as a ratio on the page comes first, then the manual layer, then the higher tier in the machine-read layer; in practice there were no duplicates. Rows whose denominator fell back to the Return No. 2 receipts are not used. Expenditure per train mile in the returns for 1888 to 1895 is not comparable with the format from 1896 on, and was not transcribed for 1893 to 1895, so those years are not split.
