Leimma

A hundred volumes of the Istanbul Islamic court registers: in what capacity people appeared, where they lived, and how land and people were linked

The Islamic courts of Istanbul left registers from the sixteenth to the nineteenth century, of which a hundred volumes have been published, each with several hundred entries recording what the people who came to sue, contract or report called themselves, which quarter they lived in and which plot of land they bought or sold. Split all hundred volumes entry by entry and extract names, addresses, status and plots, and one can ask in what capacity a person appeared in court, as a resident of a quarter, a guild member, someone's slave or someone's creditor, and through whose hands a plot passed over decades.

Where it stands：Volume one (Üsküdar, volume 5) has been split entry by entry and checked, and field extraction is running across the volume; the first piece written from it, The Runaway Slave of Üsküdar, is finished, and the web version made with Runo, Outside the Wall, is online; material on a second man, Timurhan, keeper of stray livestock, has been prepared for one section

Sigma

Ekumene: a world simulation that can be run again and again

Historical explanations often say that some condition brought about some outcome, but history happens only once and there is no control group. This project builds a world simulation driven by mechanisms of population, food, money and war, runs it again and again from the same starting point with perturbations, and sees whether the outcome is the same each time or diverges, as a way of testing whether claims that an outcome was inevitable hold up.

Where it stands：The main strands of population, money, land tenure and war have been checked for internal consistency; work now is on converting epidemic and rebellion contagion into numbers of people, estimating pastoral output, and classifying transfers of territory. A long-term project with no end date

How unexpected was the war to the London bond market in 1914?

Before war broke out in July 1914, did the London bond market sense that war was coming? British and Russian government bond prices, as printed day by day in The Economist, are converted into the probability of war implied by the market; over the same period a language model that has read only pre-war texts gives its own probability, and the two curves are set side by side to see which rises first and by how much.

Where it stands：Two segments of the bond-price transcription are complete and checked, the third is under way; the language-model side is not yet connected, with two candidate models to choose from. A prediction has been registered; results by the end of 2026

An explanation of great political upheavals: why they are hard to foresee and afterwards seem inevitable

Before a political crisis breaks out, those involved can seldom tell whether it will escalate into war; afterwards, historians often say the outcome was all but unavoidable. This project pairs crises by the conditions said to drive escalation, such as military strength, alliances and geography, to see whether the share that actually escalated to war is markedly higher; it then has a language model that has read only texts from before the starting point act as an observer after the fact, judging how hard the outcome was to avoid, and compares that judgment with the real spread obtained by simulating the same starting point thirty times, to see whether it underestimates how far history could have diverged.

Where it stands：Approved in September 2026; none of the three first-stage tasks, counting past crises, making the paired comparison and running the language model's judgment, has begun. Two predictions have been registered; results by the end of 2026

Peasant living standards and the burden of taxes and dues

What share of a peasant household's output went in taxes and dues (land tax, poll tax, labour service, church tithes and the like), and how did that share relate to whether the household had enough to eat and anything to spare? For each place, local yields, rates of levy and subsistence standards of the time are taken to work out the actual burden and surplus; every step of the reckoning cites its source, and no single national conclusion is drawn.

Where it stands：Ninety-two places are being worked through one by one: fifteen have complete accounts on both the levy and the output side, thirty-five have half, and forty-two still lack material on output, which is being gathered. The text awaits final review; due at the end of November 2026

Hard and good years in pawnshop ledgers

When a pawnshop took a pledge it recorded, item by item, the object, the sum lent, the household, the date and whether it was redeemed. When a family pawned its padded jacket, when it redeemed it, from which year it never redeemed it again: linked household by household, these give the family's good and bad years without borrowing official rice prices or average wages.

Where it stands：Since approval no ledger kept day by day and household by household has been found; what has turned up are pawnshop regulations and summary accounts, not item-by-item entries. If no ledger is in hand by the end of September, the project will be set aside

Which kinds of alliance did the Romans call a crime?

In the late Republic the Senate called private political combinations factio and coniuratio, words of abuse; under the Empire, emperors regulated associations (collegia). What I want to establish is which of the ways people joined together, alliance, association, friendship, faction, conspiracy, Roman writers treated as a crime, and how that judgment changed from the late Republic to the early and middle Empire. The material is Latin writing from the first century BCE to the second century CE, with inscriptions and legal texts.

Where it stands：The measurement of praise and blame across the three periods has been calibrated; retrieving and collating the inscriptions and other material is under way
