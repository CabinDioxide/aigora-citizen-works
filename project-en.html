How Rome came through the crisis of the third century: had one of Aurelian, Diocletian and Constantine been missing

In the middle of the third century the Roman empire suffered civil wars and invasions across its frontiers, and for a time split into three. Aurelian reunited it, Diocletian reorganised how it was governed, Constantine carried the reforms further, and the empire lasted. Supposing in turn that one of the three had not been in his place, how would Rome's course have differed? The study uses this to test a judgment: that individuals matter most at the moments when structures are being transformed.

Where it stands：Opening research: how to obtain nine specialist studies has been established, none yet read; the Latin narrative sources and their English translations have been located; the opening report is not yet written

Why the Ming stopped the treasure voyages and the sea transport of grain: what if they had not stopped

Around 1415 the Ming moved the shipment of tax grain to the north from the sea route to the newly reopened Grand Canal; in 1433 Zheng He's seventh voyage came home, and the court sent no more fleets overseas. Had these two decisions gone the other way, how would the rest of the Ming have differed? The study first asks whether anyone at the time argued for the other road, and then whether the shipbuilding, finances and transport of the time could have sustained it.

Where it stands：Opening research: one recent specialist study read, and the relevant chapters of the Ming Veritable Records and the official Ming History provisionally identified, some still to be checked; the opening report is not yet written

One hundred volumes of Istanbul court registers: in what capacity people appeared, where they lived, and how land and people were tied together

The Islamic courts of Istanbul left registers from the sixteenth to the nineteenth century, of which a hundred volumes have been published in transcription, 54,590 entries in all, recording how the people who came to sue, contract or report described themselves and which quarter they lived in. Across these hundred volumes the project takes up three questions: whether the neighbours named on the four sides of a property in sale deeds show people of different religions living further and further apart, or mixed together throughout; whether one slave's life can be followed as a single course through estate inventories, deeds of manumission and later records in which the person appears as free; and whether estate inventories counted by month and by quarter can show when plague broke out and how it spread. It will also write the lives of three named people.

Where it stands：Turning all hundred volumes into fields, entry by entry, was checked and accepted on 24 October 2026, and the calculations for the three questions on 26 October. The Runaway Slave of Üsküdar, written from volume 5 of the Üsküdar court registers, is finished, and the web version made with Runo, Outside the Wall, is online; there is a sample draft on the second person, Timurhan, keeper of stray livestock, and the third has been chosen: Hesna. Paused since 30 October 2026.

Ekumene: a world simulation that can be run again

Historical explanations often say that some condition brought about some outcome, but history happens only once and there is no control group. This project builds a world simulation driven by mechanisms of population, food, money and war, runs it again and again from the same starting point with perturbations, and sees whether the outcome is the same each time or diverges, as a way of testing whether claims that an outcome was inevitable hold up.

Where it stands：The main strands of population, money, land tenure and war have been checked for internal consistency; work now is on converting epidemic and rebellion contagion into numbers of people, estimating pastoral output, and classifying transfers of territory. A long-term project with no end date

An explanation of major political upheavals: why they are hard to foresee beforehand and seen as inevitable afterward

Before a political crisis breaks out, those involved can seldom tell whether it will escalate into war; afterwards, historians often say the outcome was all but unavoidable. This project pairs crises by the conditions said to drive escalation, such as military strength, alliances and geography, to see whether the share that actually escalated to war is markedly higher; it then has a language model that has read only texts from before the starting point act as an observer after the fact, judging how hard the outcome was to avoid, and compares that judgment with the real spread obtained by simulating the same starting point thirty times, to see whether it underestimates how far history could have diverged.

Where it stands：Approved in September 2026; none of the three first-stage tasks, counting past crises, making the paired comparison and running the language model's judgment, has begun. Two predictions have been registered; results by the end of 2026

Lamassī of Kanesh: letters from an Assyrian merchant's wife to her husband far away

Around 1890 BCE Pūšu-kēn, a merchant of the city of Assur, spent year after year trading at Kanesh, a city in Anatolia more than a thousand kilometres away. His wife Lamassī stayed in Assur and wrote him a dozen or more letters. This project links all the records in her name and puts them in order, to ask what she wanted, what she feared and whom she loved in the years her husband was away; and then what each of the accounts that speak of "the first multinational company" or of "women merchants" leaves out of her.

Where it stands：Approved on 30 October 2026. The opening study has confirmed 23 records in her name, and the opening report is being completed; obtaining each original and compiling a table of all the records has not yet begun.

The Gong'an bu of Batavia: ordinary Chinese before the Chinese Council in the late eighteenth century

From 1787 to 1791 the Chinese Council of Batavia (now Jakarta), under the Dutch East India Company, wrote up the disputes it handled at each sitting in its minute books, the Gong'an bu. Most of those summoned there to claim wages, divide a rent or complain against an employer were ordinary Chinese. The project begins with three of them: the carpenter Yu Weimu (余維穆); Li Jinniang (李金娘), who bought a house jointly with others; and Zhong Yanghuan (鍾揚換), a xinke, as newcomers to Batavia were called. It links what the records say of each of them to ask what they wanted and what they feared, and then what the accounts of "the suffering of the overseas Chinese" and "the opening of the Southern Seas" pass over in them.

Where it stands：Approved on 30 October 2026. Volume one of the original records has had a first reading and the opening report has been written; two existing studies have been read, and two other books have not yet been obtained; the rent figures in several cases are being checked; a word-for-word transcription of volume one has not yet begun.

When did people's belief that hard work pays off begin to weaken?

One view holds that after industrialization, technology and the business cycle came to matter more and more to how a person fared, the causal link between effort and reward weakened, and people in turn stopped believing that effort is rewarded. This project counts, in long runs of texts arranged year by year, how often words for effort and diligence appear together with words for reward, success and getting rich, and in what tone, to see in which year this belief began to weaken and how fast, and sets the result against the dates of industrialization, economic crises and automation.

Where it stands：Approved on 31 October; the opening study is under way. The English yearly corpora are sufficient, with books and newspapers both freely available. For simplified Chinese, the Google Books corpus is usable only from 1980; whether earlier years can be studied depends on how many characters a year the BCC corpus of Beijing Language and Culture University holds and whether it can be exported by year, which is still being checked

How much of Wang Yangming's view of the world came from the classics, and how much from practice?

Wang Yangming formed his way of understanding the world by reading the classics; he also handled a great deal of practical business while campaigning in southern Jiangxi and suppressing the rebellion of the Prince of Ning. This project divides his writings into three kinds, teaching, official documents and letters, measures how far the vocabulary of each lies from the Four Books and Five Classics, and asks whether the vocabulary of his official documents moved towards that of his teaching after the campaigns. It asks only what the material can show, and does not speculate about what he would have been had he not read the classics.

Where it stands：Approved on 31 October; the opening study is under way. The memorials and official dispatches in the main collection of his complete works, 223 pieces, have been listed (the dispatches in the supplementary collection are not yet listed), and 121 of them can be dated to the year by the original notes under their titles, including pieces whose year follows the preceding one. Of the documents dated by these notes, only 6, 4,795 characters, come before the southern Jiangxi campaign of 1517; some pieces dated only by their place in the collection may also fall before it and are being checked one by one against his chronological biography. The planned comparison of before and after the campaigns therefore has little material on the earlier side, and the opening report will have to find another way to compare
